**The short answer for parents: a general business degree with no specialization, no placement and no analytical depth is one of the weaker bets in this index.** The versions that work are specific: strong co-op programs, a specialization with structural protection, genuine quantitative depth, or an explicit route into operations or ownership.

## Will AI replace business analysts?

It is already displacing a substantial share of the work, and unlike most professions there is little structural protection to slow it down.

| AI is taking | It can't touch |
|---|---|
| Report building and dashboard maintenance | Owning a number and answering for it |
| Deck assembly and formatting | Managing people |
| Market and competitor research | Negotiating with someone who has different interests |
| Process documentation | Deciding what should be measured at all |
| Requirements gathering and write-up | Judgment when the data is incomplete and the call is expensive |
| Meeting notes, trackers, status reporting | Being accountable when the quarter misses |

The pattern in business is unusually clean: **almost everything on the left is what an entry-level business graduate was hired to do.**

Business has no licensing protection, no physical requirement, and accountability that only arrives with seniority. What protection exists has to be earned rather than inherited from the profession.

### One factor, worked through in full

*So you can see what the analysis actually looks like.*

**How hard will it be to land that first job? — rated 7.5, and the mirror image of nursing**

This factor is the clearest illustration in the whole index of why we score entry separately from the job itself.

Compare two professions. Nursing rates **2.0** here — its on-ramp is legally mandated, since a student cannot qualify without supervised clinical hours, and no employer can decide otherwise. Business rates **7.5**, and the difference is not about how useful the work is. It is that nothing protects it.

The classic business first rungs — analyst programs, coordinator roles, junior project management — are precisely the automatable layer. Firms that once hired three graduates to build the reports now hire one experienced person with tooling. Nothing in law or professional regulation prevents that, so nothing has.

**And there is a second pressure our framework does not capture at all.** Business is the most-taken degree in the country, which means entry-level roles face plain oversupply alongside automation. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York puts recent-graduate underemployment at ==-41.5% — against 12.8% for nursing, the lowest of any major it tracks==. Two forces compressing the same rung, only one of which we measure. We flag this as a limitation rather than pretending the score covers it.

The consequence is specific and it is what makes business harder than its scores suggest. The destination jobs — management, P&L ownership, operations leadership — are genuinely protected at 4.9 to 5.0. But you historically reached them *through* the exposed roles. **The ladder's top is intact. Its bottom is thinning. And nobody has yet built a replacement route.**

## Which business careers are safest from AI?

Ranked by exposure, safest first:

1. **General management / P&L — 4.9.** Accountability for outcomes and for people is the protection, and it is real.
2. **Supply chain & operations — 5.0.** Leans physical, involves real-world constraints, and has proven stubbornly resistant.
3. **HR / people — 5.3.** Human judgment, conflict, and legal accountability, though administrative HR is heavily exposed.
4. **Project management — 6.1.** Coordination is automatable; accountability for delivery is not.
5. **Business analyst / coordinator — 7.6.** The exposed layer, and the traditional way in.

## Is a business degree still worth it in 2026?

It depends almost entirely on what version of it you take.

The weak version is a general business degree with no specialization, no placement, and no quantitative depth, pointed at an analyst job. On our scoring that is close to marketing in risk, and it is what a large share of business students are actually enrolled in.

The versions that hold up:

- **Strong co-op or placement programs**, where the experience substitutes for the vanishing analyst rung
- **Operations and supply chain**, which lean physical and score meaningfully better
- **Genuine analytical rigor** — the judgment layer rather than the reporting layer
- **An explicit entrepreneurial track**, since building something is the one business activity that cannot be entry-level-automated

The question worth putting to any business program: **what job title do your graduates hold at year one, and at year five?** The gap between those two answers is the whole story, and most programs have never been asked.

## Common questions

**Will AI replace business analysts?**
It is already displacing much of the work — requirements gathering, documentation, reporting and dashboard maintenance are among the most automatable tasks we score. The role is not disappearing so much as its entry floor is rising.

**Is a business degree useless now?**
No, but it is the field where the specific program matters most and the general version matters least. Placement, specialization and analytical depth are what separate the outcomes.

**What is the safest business career?**
General management with real P&L accountability, at 4.9, followed by supply chain and operations at 5.0. Both are protected by ownership of outcomes rather than by any structural feature of the profession.

**Is an MBA still worth it?**
Our index scores the work rather than the credential, but the pattern is suggestive: the protected destinations are all accountability roles, and an MBA is one of the more direct routes into them. It does not protect the analyst tier below.

**Is business safer than computer science?**
At entry, marginally — 7.6 against 8.1, both high. At senior level business management (4.9) and software architecture (5.4) are close. Neither field protects its own entry route.
