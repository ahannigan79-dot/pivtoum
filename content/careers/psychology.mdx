**The short answer, if you work in psychology: the licensed path is excellent, and everything short of it is ordinary graduate exposure.** The question that matters for your own security is not whether psychology is safe — it is how far along the licensed path your work actually sits.

## Will AI replace therapists?

Not legally, and probably not clinically. But there is a subtler question underneath, and it is the most interesting problem in this profile.

<VersusGrid
  taking={["Session notes and documentation","Intake screening and scheduling","Progress tracking and outcome measures","Psychoeducational material","Standardized assessment scoring"]}
  untouched={["The therapeutic relationship, which *is* the treatment","Sitting with someone in genuine distress","Judgment when a client is at risk","Legal and ethical accountability for a patient","Reading what a person isn't saying"]}

Regulation is actively reinforcing this. Several US states have moved to prohibit AI from delivering licensed therapy — one of very few places in this index where the law is moving *toward* a profession rather than away from it.

**The harder question is volume rather than practice.** "Therapy and companionship" is now among the most common uses of general-purpose AI, and a substantial share of young people report using chatbots for mental health support — mostly without telling anyone. None of that competes with a licensed therapist under the same rules. It may, over time, change how many people seek professional help at all.

Our framework measures whether a human must do the work. It does not measure how many humans are needed. That is a real limitation and we state it in the full profile.

## Why does the licensed path score so low? The six factors

<WorkedExample>

This is the highest rating we give any career on this factor, and it is worth understanding why, because it explains both the strength and the limit of psychology's protection.

Two things are bundled inside this factor. **Trust**: does the work require a person to be present in a way another person can rely on? **Accountability**: must a human own the outcome when something goes wrong?

Therapy scores at the top of both. The relationship is not a delivery mechanism for the treatment — it *is* the treatment, in a way that has no parallel elsewhere in this index. A surgeon's patient wants competence; a therapy client needs to be known by someone. And when a client is at risk of harm, a licensed human carries duty-of-care obligations that no system can hold.

That combination is why the licensed score sits at 3.5 despite a fairly high automatability rating of 6.8. A great deal of what fills a clinician's week — notes, screening, scheduling, outcome measures — is genuinely automatable. It just isn't the part that matters.

**But note precisely what this protects.** It protects the practice: the requirement that a licensed human performs the work. It does not protect the volume of that work. Someone using a chatbot at 2am instead of booking an appointment has not breached any regulation, and our framework cannot see them.

That is the single clearest gap in our methodology, and psychology is where it shows.

## Which psychology careers are safest from AI?

Ranked by exposure, safest first:

1. **Clinical psychologist — 3.5.** Maximum trust, comprehensive licensure, continuous novelty.
2. **Counselor / psychotherapist — 3.9.** Same protections, slightly broader practice variation.
3. **School psychologist — 4.0.** Adds physical presence and institutional accountability; persistent shortages.
4. **Research / academic — 6.5.** Analysis, writing and literature work are heavily automatable, and academic entry is fiercely competitive.
5. **Organizational / HR-adjacent — 7.0.** Generalist office work with no licensing protection.
6. **Degree only — 7.1.** Leads to the general graduate market, which carries ordinary exposure.

## What this means if you're already in the field

Where your work sits on the licensed path is what the score is really about, and it deserves a straight answer.

The protected destinations — clinical psychology, counseling, school psychology, therapy — sit behind a license, and the protection is theirs. If your work is licensed clinical practice, your exposure is among the lowest in this index. If it sits in the unlicensed band — organizational, HR-adjacent, generalist office roles — it carries ordinary graduate-market exposure, and closing the distance to the license is the single highest-leverage move for your own security.

That distance is real. Around 14% of psychology bachelor's holders go on to graduate study in psychology — so most people with a psychology background are not in licensed practice at all, and the general graduate market they sit in is exposed like any other.
