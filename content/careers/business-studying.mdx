**The short answer, if you're weighing whether to study business: a general degree with no specialization, no placement and no analytical depth is one of the weaker bets in this index.** The versions that work are specific: strong co-op programs, a specialization with structural protection, genuine quantitative depth, or an explicit route into operations or ownership.

## Why does business score the way it does? The six factors

How a business analyst rates against each. Ratings are 0–10 on each factor's own terms.

<FactorList />

Business has no licensing protection, no physical requirement, and accountability that only arrives with seniority. What protection exists has to be earned rather than inherited from the profession.

<WorkedExample>

This factor is the clearest illustration in the whole index of why we score entry separately from the job itself.

Compare two professions. Nursing rates **2.0** here — its on-ramp is legally mandated, since a student cannot qualify without supervised clinical hours, and no employer can decide otherwise. Business rates **7.5**, and the difference is not about how useful the work is. It is that nothing protects it.

The classic business first rungs — analyst programs, coordinator roles, junior project management — are precisely the automatable layer. Firms that once hired three graduates to build the reports now hire one experienced person with tooling. Nothing in law or professional regulation prevents that, so nothing has.

**And there is a second pressure our framework does not capture at all.** Business is the most-taken degree in the country, which means entry-level roles face plain oversupply alongside automation. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York puts recent-graduate underemployment at **41.5% — against 12.8% for nursing, the lowest of any major it tracks**. Two forces compressing the same rung, only one of which we measure. We flag this as a limitation rather than pretending the score covers it.

The consequence is specific and it is what makes business harder than its scores suggest. The destination jobs — management, P&L ownership, operations leadership — are genuinely protected at 4.9 to 5.0. But you historically reached them *through* the exposed roles. **The ladder's top is intact. Its bottom is thinning. And nobody has yet built a replacement route.**

</WorkedExample>

## Which business careers are safest from AI?

Ranked by exposure, safest first:

1. **General management / P&L — 4.9.** Accountability for outcomes and for people is the protection, and it is real.
2. **Supply chain & operations — 5.0.** Leans physical, involves real-world constraints, and has proven stubbornly resistant.
3. **HR / people — 5.3.** Human judgment, conflict, and legal accountability, though administrative HR is heavily exposed.
4. **Project management — 6.1.** Coordination is automatable; accountability for delivery is not.
5. **Business analyst / coordinator — 7.6.** The exposed layer, and the traditional way in.

## Is a business degree still worth it in 2026?

It depends almost entirely on which version of it you take.

The weak version — a general degree with no specialization or placement, pointed at an analyst job — also carries a plain oversupply problem on top of the automation one, and that non-AI caution is worth weighing separately.
