**The short answer, if you work in cybersecurity: better than most technical paths, and not as protected as the reputation suggests.** Nothing in this field scores in the low band. What protects it is an adversary who is actively trying to be unpredictable — and the roles that face that adversary directly are not the ones you start out in.

## Why is cybersecurity moderately protected? The six factors

How incident response and threat hunting rates against each. Ratings are 0–10 on each factor's own terms.

<FactorList />

<WorkedExample>

AI is strong on patterns and weak on genuine novelty. Most careers earn a high rating on this factor because their situations are complex — a one-off engineering site, an atypical patient, a case with no precedent.

**Cybersecurity earns it because someone is deliberately manufacturing novelty against you.**

That distinction matters more than it sounds. An unusual medical presentation is unusual by accident. An unusual attack is unusual *on purpose*, designed specifically to look like something benign or to exploit the gap between what a detection system was trained on and what is actually happening.

This creates a property no other profession in this index has: **the novelty is adaptive.** Whatever pattern a defensive system learns to recognize, an adversary has an incentive to stop matching it. There is no equilibrium where the patterns settle down and the work becomes routine, because the other side is paid to prevent exactly that.

That is why threat hunting rates 9.0 and holds a 5.0 score despite no physical component, no licensure, and a fully digital workflow. Almost nothing else in this index survives that combination of missing protections.

**But notice where the protection does not reach.** A tier-one SOC analyst is not facing adversarial novelty. They are working a queue of alerts against a playbook — pattern-matching, in a role explicitly designed to be pattern-matching, which is why it rates 4.5 rather than 9.0 and scores 7.4.

**The general lesson: adversarial work resists automation better than complex work.** Complexity can be learned. An opponent who changes when you learn cannot be. Any career with a genuine adversary — security, litigation, fraud investigation, negotiation, competitive strategy — carries a protection that complexity alone does not provide.

Ranked by exposure, safest first:

2. **Security architecture — 5.5.** System-level judgment where being wrong is expensive.
3. **Penetration testing — 5.7.** Creative adversarial work, though tooling automates much of the routine scanning.
4. **GRC / compliance — 6.1.** Regulatory weight, but substantial documentation and evidence-collection content.
5. **SOC analyst tier 1 — 7.4.** Playbook-driven triage, and the traditional entry route.

## What this means if you're already in the field

The move the score points at is the same one every ranking above implies: toward the adversarial end and away from the queue. The field has genuine demand and the senior roles carry real protection — but that safety lives in incident response, threat hunting and architecture, not in tier-one SOC work, which is the most automated part of it. That is the same entry-path problem that afflicts software, law, finance and accounting: the profession is healthy and the on-ramp is narrowing.

If your day is still mostly playbook-driven triage, that's the tier the automation is clearing fastest, and closing the distance to genuinely adversarial work is the single highest-leverage thing you can do for your own security. What protects you is proximity to the adversary — the work where someone is deliberately manufacturing novelty against you — rather than proximity to the tooling.

What actually holds up has less to do with the score than with how deep into that adversarial work you go. The full guide covers where the durable roles sit, which versions of the path hold up, and how to judge whether your own work is building adversarial thinking or just tool operation.
