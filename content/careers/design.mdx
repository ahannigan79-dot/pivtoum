**The short answer for parents: not for production work.** A design degree aimed at making assets is the single riskiest path we score. It is defensible aimed explicitly at creative direction, at UX and product design, or at design paired with a domain — but those are different destinations that require different training.

It has already replaced a substantial share of what production designers were paid to do.

The distinction is between *making* and *directing*. A generative system can produce a thousand options. It cannot decide which one is right for this brand, this market, this moment — and it cannot be accountable for the choice.

## Why does design score so high? The six factors

How production and execution design rates against each. Ratings are 0–10 on each factor's own terms.

<FactorList />

<WorkedExample>

Everywhere else in this index, the automatable layer is the administrative shell around the work. Nurses chart. Doctors write notes. Teachers mark. Lawyers draft. In every one of those cases, automation removes something people resented and leaves the work they trained for.

**Design is the exception, and it is why this profession feels different from the inside.** The automatable layer here *is* the craft. Making the image, building the layout, producing the asset — that was not the paperwork around design. It was design.

That is what a 9.1 means. Not that the profession is worthless, but that the specific activity most designers spend most of their day performing is now something a non-designer can do adequately in seconds.

We rate it 9.1 rather than 10 because output quality still varies enormously with the skill of the person directing it, and because production at a professional standard still requires judgment about what "good" looks like. But that judgment is a *directing* skill, not a *making* skill — and directing is a smaller job that fewer people are needed for.

**This is also why our creative direction track sits at 5.6 rather than 8.4.** Direction was never the automatable layer. It was always taste, intent and accountability, and none of those have moved much.

**The general lesson, and it applies well beyond design: ask whether the automatable part of a job is the part you would enjoy.** In most professions it isn't, and automation is a gift. In this one it is, and that changes the honest advice from "the field is fine" to "the field is fine and the job you pictured may not be."

</WorkedExample>

## Which design careers are safest from AI?

Ranked by exposure, safest first:

1. **Creative direction / brand identity — 5.6.** Taste, intent, and accountability for the choice.
2. **UX / product design — 6.0.** Research, systems thinking and sitting closer to the build.
3. **Motion, 3D and technical craft — 7.0.** Higher technical barrier, though generative tools are advancing fast here.
4. **Illustration / commercial art — 7.5.** Distinctive personal style holds value; commodity illustration does not.
5. **Production / execution design — 8.4.** The most exposed work we score.

## The strongest argument against our score

It deserves stating properly, because it has history on its side.

When desktop publishing arrived, designers feared replacement by "anyone with a mouse." The field expanded instead, and produced entirely new subfields — UX, digital branding, product design — that had not previously existed. On this reading, generative AI is the next iteration of a tool shift the profession has repeatedly absorbed, and our 8.4 mistakes a transition for a collapse.

We take it seriously, and it is part of why our direction track sits at 5.6 rather than 8.

But there is a difference worth naming between a tool that changes *how* work is produced and one that produces the work itself. Photoshop still needed a designer at the keyboard for every asset. Generation does not.

The honest position is that we do not know whether new creative categories will appear fast enough to absorb the displaced production tier. History says probably something will, and offers no guarantee about timing. **A graduate needs a job in the meantime, and the transition is exactly when the pain lands.**

## Is a design degree still worth it in 2026?

Only in specific forms.

The version that does not work is a design degree aimed at production employment. That is what a large share of design programs still train for, and it is the highest-scoring career in this index.

Which versions do work — the programs, the adjacent tracks, and the domain pairings where the training still buys something — along with the routes into each and how the portfolio question has changed now that polished volume signals nothing, are what the full guide sets out.
