# I build AI for a living. Here's what I'm telling my own kid.

### Why we built The AI Career Index.

*Adam, founder · Pivotum · Fall 2026*

---

I'm going to be honest with you in a way that probably isn't good marketing: I am scared of what the future holds for this generation of children. Not in the abstract. For my child.

Look at the world we're handing them. It moves faster than the one we grew up in and holds still for no one. The basic footholds — an affordable place to live, a degree that reliably pays off, a stable first job — cost more and promise less than they did a generation ago. The pace of change has outrun the advice we were raised on for keeping up with it. Every generation inherits its parents' unfinished business; this one inherits more of it, faster, than most.

And now, arriving faster than anything before it, comes artificial intelligence — landing on your child's shoulders at the exact moment they're choosing a major.

Here's the strange part. AI has a genuine shot at easing every one of those pressures: new medicine, new energy, more done with less, hours of life handed back. It could be the thing that *lightens* the load this generation carries rather than one more weight on the pile. It is the fire and the fire extinguisher at once.

Which is the whole reason I'm writing this. Left to run on its own, AI quietly becomes one more item on the list of things our kids never asked for. I can't fix the rest of that list — no parent can. But this one we have a say in. On the single decision in front of us right now — what our children point their working lives at — we can make sure AI is something we saw coming, not something that blindsided them. **That's the aim: don't let AI be one more thing on the list. Not here. Not for your kid.**

## The reason it won't slow down

That promise is exactly why nothing is going to slow this down.

The lure of solving our biggest problems will underwrite AI's expansion indefinitely. More time in our lives. Cheaper healthcare. A functioning grid. Those are not small prizes, and they are dangled in front of every government and every board in the world. So AI will end up everywhere, whether it does good or harm, because the upside is too attractive to refuse and the constraint we probably should show is expensive, slow, and politically unrewarding.

I don't say that as a critic standing outside it. I build AI systems for a living. I'm on the front lines of AI transformation and my job depends on this wave continuing. Which is why the next sentence should carry some weight.

I am not convinced the benefits will arrive the way we're all assuming they will. I would love to be wrong about that. What I have no doubt about at all is this: within our lifetimes, and I mean within the next five years, the job and career landscape will be changed beyond recognition.

## It won't look like robots

When people picture AI taking work, they picture machines doing what a person used to do. There will be some of that — I'll admit a laundry-folding robot sits near the top of my personal wish list. But the change that will actually reshape your child's career is quieter than that, and far more erosive. The job doesn't disappear. The way the job gets done shifts, and it tends to shift in a way that takes the exact thing you were hired for.

Don't believe me? Take this article.

I can't write to save my life. Never have been able to. What I did was think hard about what I believe and lay it out — the argument, the order, what mattered and what didn't, which lines were honest and which were just comfortable. Every judgment in this piece is mine. What I handed to a machine was the execution.

That's not an admission. That's the entire thesis in miniature. The thinking and the creativity were the scarce part; the craft of assembling sentences, which used to be the barrier to entry, wasn't. And you're still reading, which is rather the point.

Let me be careful here, though, because there's a lazy version of this argument that's simply wrong. Truly top-quality human writing — the kind with a voice you'd recognize blind, that makes you stop and read a line twice — is a thing of beauty and it is not being copied. I don't expect it to be. That is not what's at risk.

What's at risk is everything underneath it: the enormous middle of the profession where competent, clear, serviceable prose was the job. That's where most careers actually live — in writing and in every other field. The very top of any profession remains the safest place to stand. The problem is that nobody starts there.

Five years ago, a counselor looking at a kid with a gift for words would say: you're a strong writer, have you considered journalism? Reasonable advice. Kind advice. Today that sentence needs a hard follow-up question, because journalism is being rewritten as we speak — and the part being rewritten first is the part that kid is good at.

The skill hasn't become worthless. It has moved. Writing well is closer to table stakes now; having something worth saying and the judgment to know what's true is what's left standing. That's the pattern to watch for in every career your child is considering. Not replacement. Relocation — of where the value in the job actually sits.

## What our parents got to do, and what we don't

Our parents had it comparatively easy. They coached us toward a job for life, or a respected profession, or a degree that opened doors. The map they handed us was imperfect but it was broadly the same map they'd used themselves.

We don't have that. The map our kids need is being redrawn underneath our feet while they're filling out college applications. And so we have a duty of care that our parents never faced: we have to explain a landscape we're still learning ourselves.

I can't overstate how much I believe children need to hear this from their parents. Not from a guidance counselor who saw them for twenty minutes. Not from a school system that has mostly decided AI is a cheating problem. From us, at the exact moment they're making a decision that shapes the next decade of their life.

You do not need to understand the technology. Nobody is asking you to read a research paper. But we should all understand the mechanics — what these systems are actually good at, where they fail, and how that maps onto the shape of a career.

## The lead indicators are already here

Nobody has all the answers, including us. But the early signals are not subtle:

- **Repetitive work is going.** Not eventually. Now. If a task is structured, documented, and done the same way every time, it is on the list.
- **Entry-level channels are collapsing.** This is the one that should worry parents most. The junior work that used to build judgment — the grunt tasks, the first two years — is exactly what AI does cheapest. The ladder is losing its bottom rungs, and you can't reach the top of a profession without them.
- **Curiosity and flexibility are becoming the actual qualifications.** Not the credential. The ability to keep learning when the ground moves.

## The one piece of advice

If I could give any parent a single thing, it would be this: our kids need us to guide them more than they have ever needed it, and the stereotypes that made sense five years ago are actively dangerous now. Chuck them out. Then spend real time getting familiar with how jobs are evolving.

That's what the Index is for. We scored 28 careers and 158 individual tracks, and we show the reasoning rather than asking you to trust a number, because a score you can't interrogate is worthless when the stakes are your kid's next ten years. The scoring helps tell the story. It isn't the story.

And I'll say the unprofitable thing out loud: even if you never buy a single Profile from us, please go and get informed. Read the free ones. Read someone else's. Read anything. Ask your child what they think is coming and actually listen to the answer.

I build AI systems for a living, and I have a teenager. That's the whole reason this exists. For my own kid, and for yours.

And I intend to keep shouting about it as loudly as I can, for as long as it takes. Not because I enjoy being the person at the barbecue talking about labor markets, but because the math is simple: every parent who takes notice now, and starts preparing their child for this shift rather than the last one, is one more kid who gets to walk into it with their eyes open. That's worth being loud about.

Educate yourself. It's the most protective thing you can do for them right now.

— Adam, founder, Pivotum
