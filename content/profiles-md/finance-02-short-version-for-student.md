# So you're thinking about finance

*Written for you, not about you.*

---

We scored twenty-eight careers on how exposed they are to AI.

A financial advisor: **4.6** out of 10.

An entry-level analyst: **8.0**.

==Same degree. Finance protects the person in the room with the client, and offers almost
nothing to the person preparing the materials for that room.==

---

## The 60-second version

- The advisory and risk end holds up well. The analyst tier is among the most exposed work we score.
- ==-A first-year analyst's job description reads like a list of automated tasks.==
- **The timeline is the thing nobody tells you:** banking applications open up to 18 months early.
- Everyone applies to investment banking. Far fewer apply to the roles that score better and hire more easily.
- One honest caveat: finance is cyclical, and we can't fully separate that from AI.

---

## Read this bit first — it's time-sensitive

If investment banking is even slightly of interest, you need to know this now rather than in
third year.

==-Summer analyst applications open up to eighteen months in advance.== And full-time offers
go primarily to people who did the internship.

**Which means the decision effectively happens in your first year of university, not your
third.** Not the application — the *preparation*. Networking, target firms, application dates.

Plenty of capable people miss this entirely and find out when it's too late. That's the single
most useful thing in this document.

---

## What AI actually took

Financial modeling. Scenario building. Company and sector research. Earnings analysis and
comparables. Deck production. Data cleaning. First-draft memos.

==-That isn't *adjacent* to what a first-year analyst does. It's a fairly complete description
of the job.==

What it can't touch: sitting with someone making the biggest financial decision of their life.
Judging a risk with no historical precedent. Being the person who gets called when markets
fall. Carrying regulatory responsibility for advice.

---

## The scores

| Track | Score |
|---|---|
| Wealth / financial advisory | 4.6 |
| Risk & compliance | 5.3 |
| Investment banking (senior) | 5.7 |
| Corporate finance / FP&A | 6.8 |
| Entry analyst / research | 8.0 |

==Exposure falls as personal accountability rises.== That's the whole map, and it holds across
every career we scored.

**Which is awkward**, because you can't start with accountability. It has to be earned — and
the roles where people traditionally earned it are exactly the ones being compressed.

---

## The bit nobody applies to

Here's something genuinely useful.

Almost everyone your age applies to investment banking. Application volumes are at records,
and hiring is reportedly strongest at *associate and VP* level — meaning firms are buying
experience rather than training it.

Meanwhile ==+wealth advisory, risk and compliance score better, hire more readily, and have
far fewer applicants.==

Risk and compliance in particular is growing with regulatory expansion and struggling to fill.
It's not glamorous. It's protected, and it's a real career.

**Worth at least looking at, rather than defaulting to the thing everyone else wants.**

---

## The honest caveat

We'd rather tell you this than let a confident number sit there.

==?Finance is the most cyclical career we score.== Deal activity collapsed in 2023–24 and
analyst hiring collapsed with it. Both have recovered somewhat.

So some of the movement we measured is the cycle, not AI, and **we can't cleanly separate
them.**

What keeps our score high is what happened to the *content* of the job rather than the number
of seats. Major firms now interview for whether you can **direct** AI through modeling and
research, rather than build the model yourself. When a profession redesigns what a first-year
is *for*, the apprenticeship has changed regardless of how many chairs exist.

You can get the analyst seat and still not get the training the seat used to provide.

---

## The skill nobody's teaching

Knowing when the model is wrong.

==+You can only spot an implausible number if you understand the business well enough — which
is exactly what the old years of building models by hand used to teach you.==

So the fundamentals matter *more*, not less. The difference is that nobody's going to make you
learn them the slow way any more, which means you have to want to.

---

## What we won't soften

**The hours at the banking end are as bad as you've heard**, and they're worst in your
twenties.

**It's geographically concentrated.** Hiring clusters in a few financial centers. That's a life
decision, not just a career one.

**Competition at entry is extreme** — record application volumes against selective hiring.

**And people drawn primarily by the money or the prestige leave in years two and three at high
rates.** That's not a moral point. It's just that the hours only make sense if something else
about the work holds you.

---

## Does this actually sound like you?

Finance suits people who:

- Are **comfortable with consequence** — being wrong costs money belonging to someone else
- Can hold **detail and narrative at once** — the numbers and what they actually mean
- Are **genuinely commercially curious**, not just good at maths
- **Handle sustained pressure** rather than occasional pressure
- Can **build trust with people much older and wealthier** — that's the advisory core, and
  it's more testable than you'd think

Harder if you want predictable hours early, need geographic flexibility, or are mostly being
pulled by prestige.

---

## What actually matters when picking a course

- **Internship placement rate, and with whom.** Full-time offers flow from internships, so
  this is the number that matters most.
- ==+**When does careers support start?** If it's second year, that's already late for
  banking.==
- **Real quantitative depth**, compulsory rather than optional
- **Does it place into advisory, risk and corporate finance** — or only into the
  oversubscribed end?

---

## Two things worth doing

**Talk to someone in wealth advisory or risk**, not just banking. The picture is completely
different and almost nobody your age has heard it.

**Then find someone two or three years into finance** and ask what they actually did in year
one — and whether that work still exists in the same form.

---

## One comparison worth making

**Accounting.** Licensed CPA advisory scores 5.2 against entry finance at 8.0.

The pay is worse — a first-year audit associate earns $55–70K against $85–100K in banking.
But it comes with a license, a severe shortage, and a qualification pathway that ==+43 states
have just made shorter and cheaper.==

Different trade-off. Worth understanding rather than dismissing.
