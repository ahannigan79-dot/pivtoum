## Before you read this — a note for parents

You're likely reading this before your child is. That's normal, and it's worth pausing on how
you use it.

**Don't lead with the scores.** If you open with a risk number, they either get frightened and
shut down, or defensive about a decision they've already made.

**What this is.** A facts document, not a recommendation.

**A practical note:** there's a separate short version written directly to them. Send them
that one.

**And one thing specific to marketing.** ==?This is the profile where the displacement is
hardest to see, because it isn't happening through layoffs.== Only about 11% of organisations
report replacing workers with AI — but three-quarters of marketers now report doing the work
of more than one job. Nobody is being fired. Positions simply aren't being refilled, and the
work is redistributed to whoever is left.

That matters for how you read everything below. The headline job numbers look survivable. The
experience of being in the job has changed considerably.

# The short answer

**Not on its own.** A general marketing degree pointed at a content or campaign execution job
is one of the weakest combinations we score.

Content and social execution scores **8.2** out of 10. Brand strategy and marketing leadership
scores **5.5**.

And here's what makes marketing structurally different from law, nursing or accounting:
==marketing has no licensing protection, no physical requirement, and no structural moat of any
kind. Every bit of protection in this field has to be earned, and all of it sits at the senior
end.==

# Why marketing scores the way it does — the six factors

Here's how content and campaign execution answers them.

### How much of this job can AI already do? *(35% of the score)*

**Almost all of it.**

Copywriting and content production. Social posts and campaign variants. Email sequences and
landing pages. SEO article production. Basic design and asset resizing. Performance reporting
and dashboards.

The distinction that matters is between *producing* marketing and *deciding* marketing.
==-Generating content is no longer a job. It is a prompt.== Deciding what should exist, and
being accountable for whether it worked, still is.

### Is the path in and up being eroded? *(15%)*

**Severe, and the data is unusually specific.**

The Content Marketing Institute's 2026 survey of 644 marketers found ==-one in three companies
reducing entry-level marketing hiring, with reductions outpacing increases by roughly two and
a half to one.== Layoffs are up 30% compared with two years ago, and 75% of marketers believe
finding a job is now harder.

Meanwhile hiring for marketing roles overall is *expanding* — organisations want more
marketers. They are simply forgoing beginners for experienced candidates who can direct and
challenge AI-generated work.

### Does it have to be done in person, with your hands? *(15%)*

**None of it. Zero, permanently.**

### Does someone need a human they can trust and hold responsible? *(15%)*

**At brand leadership level meaningfully; at execution level not at all.**

A board funding a repositioning wants a human answerable for it. Nobody is accountable for a
social post.

### Does the law require a licensed human? *(10%)*

**No, and none is coming.** Section 4 explains why this matters more than it sounds.

### How often does the job hit genuinely new, high-stakes situations? *(10%)*

**At strategy level occasionally; at execution level rarely.** Repositioning a brand in a
changing market is genuine novelty. Producing the fortieth variant of an approved campaign is
not.

# Look at what a profession has at zero

Most of our worked examples explain why a career is protected. Marketing is worth examining for
the opposite reason — it is the clearest case in this index of a profession with **no
structural protection at all**, and understanding what that means is more useful than another
example of a strong score.

**Two of our six factors are effectively zero for everyone in marketing, at every level of
seniority.**

**Regulatory protection: 1.0.** There is no license. No professional body controls entry, no
statute reserves any marketing activity to a qualified human, and nothing prevents an
organisation from replacing marketing work with software the moment the software is adequate.
Compare accounting, where the audit signature is a legal monopoly, or nursing, where scope of
practice and staffing ratios are written into law.

**Physical requirement: 1.0.** All of it can be done from anywhere, which means all of it can
be done by anything.

==-Those two zeros are permanent, and they apply to the CMO as much as to the intern. Nothing
anyone does changes them.==

**So marketing's entire protection budget has to come from the remaining factors — trust,
judgment and accountability — and all three are seniority-dependent.** A brand director rates
7.0 on trust because a board wants a human answerable for the strategy. A content executive
rates 3.0 because nobody is trusting them with anything yet.

That is why the gap between the top and bottom of this profession is 2.7 points, and why the
bottom is among the highest scores in the business family.

==?The general lesson: look at what a profession has at zero. Careers with structural
protections have a floor beneath them, however junior. Careers without one have only whatever
the individual has personally earned — and a graduate has not earned anything yet.==

# The ghost workforce — how the displacement is actually happening

This is the most useful finding in the profile, and it is not what most coverage describes.

The Content Marketing Institute's 2026 Career and Salary Outlook, based on a survey of 644
full-time marketers, found something more subtle than mass replacement:

- ==+Only about **11%** of organisations report having replaced workers with AI==
- But **76%** of marketers say they are doing the work of more than one job
- And **50%** have taken on new responsibilities with no promotion or pay increase

**Nobody is being fired and replaced by a machine.** What is happening is that layoffs,
attrition and slow hiring are shrinking segments of the workforce, and the remaining work is
redistributed to whoever stays. CMI calls it a *ghost workforce* — an invisible labor pool of
marketers absorbing the roles of two or three people.

**Why this matters for a student.** ==?A profession can look healthy on headcount while the
job itself becomes materially worse.== The vacancies simply stop appearing. There is no
announcement, no restructure, and no moment anyone can point to.

It also explains the apparent contradiction in the hiring data: overall marketing hiring is
expanding while entry-level hiring contracts. Organisations want more marketers. They want
experienced ones who can direct AI work — and 43% report that role requirements have shifted
around AI usage even where headcount hasn't changed.

# What the trend shows

**Content execution: 6.5 → 7.6 → 8.2.** Up 1.7 in three years.

**Brand strategy: 4.7 → 5.0 → 5.5.** Up 0.8.

**The gap widened from 1.8 points to 2.7.** What is distinctive about marketing is that the
execution tier was *already* elevated in 2023 — text and image generation reached commercial
usability in marketing before almost anywhere else, because the quality bar for a social post
is lower than for a legal filing or a clinical note.

# How durable is the protection?

| Protection | Strength today | Durability | Where the pressure is coming from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Human trust / accountability** | Real at leadership level | **Durable** | Boards want a person answerable for brand decisions. Commercially rooted. |
| **Judgment under novelty** | Real at strategy level | **Eroding** | Repositioning resists; campaign iteration does not. |
| **Regulatory / licensing** | **None** | n/a | No license exists and none is coming. |
| **Physical / embodied** | **None** | n/a | Permanently. |

**The honest read.** ==+Marketing has two protections, both seniority-dependent, neither
inherited from the profession.== A graduate has neither, and the route to acquiring them ran
through the execution work that has been automated.

# How the role will actually change

| Was the job | Is becoming the job |
|---|---|
| Writing the copy | Editing generated copy, then judging whether it's right |
| Producing campaign variants | Choosing among generated variants |
| Building the report | Interpreting a report that arrived built |
| Learning the customer by writing to them repeatedly | Learning them… some other way |
| Deciding what the brand should mean | **Unchanged** |
| Being accountable when it doesn't work | **Unchanged** |

**The fourth row is the real problem.** ==?Marketers built judgment about what resonates by
producing a great deal of work and watching most of it fail. That was tedious and it was the
education.== Nobody has replaced it.

# Human strengths that will matter most

1. **Judgment about what's worth saying** — deciding what should exist, which is upstream of
   all production.
2. **Accountability for commercial outcomes** — owning a number and answering for it. The
   single most protective thing in this field.
3. **Reading a market that has changed** — genuine novelty, where past patterns mislead.
4. **Taking a creative risk a model wouldn't** — models regress to the mean by construction.
5. **Verification** — knowing when generated work is confidently off-brand or factually wrong.

Notably absent: writing speed, content volume and production fluency. These were the
traditional markers of a strong junior marketer and are the fastest-depreciating.

# Other things to consider about this job

## What's genuinely good about it

**The industry is not shrinking.** Global advertising spend is nearing a trillion dollars,
digital marketing roles are projected to grow around 6% through 2032, and employers posted over
376,000 marketing and creative jobs in 2025. Roughly 65% of marketing leaders planned to expand
permanent headcount into 2026.

**And the AI skill premium is real and unusually well documented.** ==+Marketers who
demonstrate genuine proficiency with AI tools report earning materially more than peers in
equivalent roles — industry salary surveys put the premium in the range of 15–30%, and
describe it as widening.==

That is one of the clearest, most actionable findings anywhere in this index: the skill gap is
currently paying.

**What marketers report as rewarding:** fast feedback — you find out whether it worked;
creative and analytical work in the same job; genuine breadth across a business; and a
relatively short path to real responsibility compared with credentialed professions.

**Specialization pays sharply.** Analytics, marketing science, product marketing and
AI-integrated roles command substantially more than generalist ones — and a marketing manager
who can run predictive analytics will out-earn a director who can't.

## What's genuinely hard about it

**The entry tier is the problem**, and it's severe.

**The workload redistribution is real and under-discussed.** Three-quarters doing more than one
job, half taking on new responsibilities without additional pay. ==-That is the actual
experience of the profession right now, and it doesn't appear in any employment statistic.==

**Career development is thin.** Only about one in four marketers reports feeling very satisfied
with career development opportunities at their organisation.

**And the title chaos is genuinely confusing.** The same work carries five different names with
enormous pay gaps between them, which makes it unusually hard for a newcomer to judge whether
an offer is good.

## Who this work suits — and who it doesn't

Marketing tends to suit people who:

- **Are comfortable being measured.** Marketing produces numbers, and the protection comes from
  owning them.
- **Are genuinely curious about why people do things** — not just about the craft of
  communication
- **Can hold creative and analytical work simultaneously**; the surviving roles need both
- **Are commercially minded** — the marketers who direct are the ones who understand the
  business objective

It's harder for people who:

- Want to **write or make things** primarily. ==?That instinct points at the automated tier,
  and it's the most common reason people choose marketing.==
- Need **clear structure and defined tasks**
- Dislike having their work **judged by results**
- Chose it because it seemed like the creative option within a business degree

## What else is moving this market

**Oversupply compounds automation.** Marketing is among the most-taken business specialisms,
with nothing capping numbers.

**Search referral traffic is collapsing for many publishers** as AI-generated answers replace
clicks, which is reshaping content marketing specifically — the discipline built on organic
search is having its foundation moved.

**And a genuinely new category is emerging.** AI marketing roles — automation directors,
AI-integrated specialists, marketing ML roles — barely existed in 2024 and now command
substantial salaries. ==+That's where the growth is, and almost nobody entering the field is
aiming at it.==

# The AI-native advantage — what to actually do about it

Marketing is where AI adoption is fastest, because nothing slows it down — no regulator, no
licensing body, no safety case. That cuts both ways: higher exposure, and a larger opening for
someone who can direct it.

**And unusually in this index, the advantage here is measurable in salary.** The 15–30% premium
for AI-fluent marketers is the most concrete evidence anywhere in this index that the AI-native
argument is not theoretical.

### What an AI-native marketer looks like

**1. Direct precisely.** Getting usable output requires knowing what good looks like, which
requires taste and commercial understanding.

**2. Verify.** Knowing when generated work is off-brand, factually wrong, or subtly tone-deaf.
==+You can only catch that if you understand the customer well enough.==

**3. Measure and own.** Everything in this profile points at accountability. Anything that puts
a graduate closer to a number they answer for moves them up the scale.

**4. Build the analytics side.** Marketing science and analytics score better and pay more.
==+The people building the models are scarcer than the people running them.==

**5. Aim at the new roles.** AI marketing automation, AI-integrated specialisms and
data-driven creative direction are the fastest-growing category in the field.

### Concrete preparation

**Before the degree:** understand that the general version of this degree is the weak one.
Pair it with analytics, a domain, or an explicit route toward commercial leadership.

**During:** take quantitative modules seriously — that's the judgment layer. Get a placement.
==+And build one real thing with a measurable outcome: a campaign, a product, a channel that
actually grew. In a field with no credential, demonstrable results are the credential.==

**Entering:** prefer roles with a number attached, and smaller organisations early where
accountability arrives sooner.

# Where a marketing degree can take them later

Broad: brand and marketing leadership, product marketing, marketing analytics and data science,
growth and performance marketing, communications and public affairs, commercial and revenue
leadership, agency leadership, and founding something.

**The pattern in this index holds precisely:** ==?roles where someone owns a commercial number
score better; roles that produce material for someone else to act on score worse.== In
marketing that distinction is unusually stark because the producing has become close to free.

# What to look for in a marketing program

**1. How much of it is quantitative, and is it compulsory?**
This is the single best predictor. Analytics is the judgment layer; content production is the
automated one.

**2. Placement — and whether it's guaranteed or competitive.**

**3. Does the curriculum teach working with AI tools *and* checking them?**
A program banning them is training for a job that no longer exists.

**4. First-destination job titles**, not employment rates. "Marketing coordinator" and
"marketing analytics manager" are very different outcomes.

**5. Is there a genuine analytics or marketing science pathway?**

---

# Questions to ask a marketing program

**On the curriculum:**
- What proportion of the degree is quantitative, and is it compulsory?
- Is there an analytics or marketing science pathway, and how many students take it?
- How does the program teach students to work with generative AI — and to verify it?
- What changed in the curriculum in the last two years?

**On placement:**
- What proportion of students complete a placement, and is it guaranteed?
- Which employers took students last year?

**On outcomes:**
- What job titles did last year's graduates hold at six months? Read the actual list.
- How has that mix changed over three years?

**Red flags:** a curriculum built around content creation and social media management;
quantitative modules optional; no placement provision; outcomes quoted as employment rate; no
engagement with AI beyond a policy about academic honesty.

# Bottom line

**Marketing is not a bad field. It is a bad default — and it is the field where the specific
version of the degree matters most.**

The destinations are genuinely good. Brand leadership, marketing science, product marketing and
the emerging AI-integrated roles are well paid, intellectually varied, and growing.

**The entry tier is the problem, and the displacement is nearly invisible.** ==Only about 11%
of organisations have replaced anyone with AI — but three-quarters of marketers are doing the
work of more than one job.== Nobody is fired. Roles simply stop being refilled.

**And marketing has no floor.** No license, no physical requirement, nothing structural. Every
bit of protection is personally earned, and a graduate starts with none of it.

**So the useful advice is:** take the quantitative modules seriously because analytics is the
judgment layer, pair marketing with a domain or with genuine analytical depth rather than
taking the general version, get accountable for a measurable number as early as possible, and
aim deliberately at the AI-integrated roles that barely existed two years ago.

And one concrete thing worth acting on. ==+The AI skill premium in marketing is currently
15–30% and widening — the clearest evidence anywhere in this index that the effort of becoming
genuinely fluent pays, and pays now rather than eventually.==

# Discussion questions

## Part 1 — About the work itself

1. What made marketing appealing? Push past "I'm creative" or "it's varied" to something specific.
2. **Do they want to make things, or decide things?** This is the diagnostic question, and the answers lead to opposite ends of the score table.
3. What does a Tuesday look like in the job they're imagining?
4. Are they comfortable being measured on results?

## Part 2 — Reacting to the findings

5. Content execution scores 8.2 and brand strategy 5.5. Does that change where they'd aim?
6. Marketing has two of six protection factors at zero, permanently. What do they make of that?
7. Only 11% of organisations have replaced anyone with AI, but 76% of marketers do the work of more than one job. Does that land?

## Part 2b — The harder conversation

8. One in three companies is cutting entry-level marketing hiring. What's the plan for getting the first job?
9. **Have you looked at first-destination job titles** from their shortlisted programs, not employment rates?
10. Is the quantitative content compulsory or optional on the courses they're considering?

## Part 2c — The other side

11. Of what marketers find rewarding — fast feedback, creative and analytical work together, breadth, early responsibility — which two matter most?
12. Looking at "who this work suits": which items sound like them? Answer separately, then compare.
13. Can they be genuinely curious about *why people do things*, rather than about the craft of communication?

## Part 2d — The AI question, practically

14. AI-fluent marketers earn 15–30% more than peers. What would becoming genuinely fluent actually involve?
15. Marketers used to build judgment by producing a lot of work and watching most of it fail. If production is automated, how do they think judgment gets built now?

## Part 3 — Testing it against reality

16. **The most valuable single action:** run something real. A campaign, a channel, a small product — anything with a measurable outcome. In a field with no credential, that's the credential.
17. Find someone two to three years into marketing and ask what they actually do, and how many jobs' worth of work it is.

## Part 4 — About the program

18. Ask each program the curriculum questions from section 13. Whether quantitative content is compulsory is the whole answer.
19. Is there a real analytics or marketing science pathway?

## Part 5 — Widening the frame

20. Have they looked at **marketing analytics or marketing science** specifically? Better score, better pay, far fewer applicants.
21. What about the emerging AI marketing roles? They barely existed in 2024 and pay substantially more.
22. If marketing weren't available, what would they choose? The answer usually reveals what they're actually drawn to.

## Part 6 — For the parent, alone

23. Is marketing the "creative but sensible" compromise here — for them, or for me?
24. Do I actually know what they'd *do* with this degree?
25. What's the one thing I most want them to understand — in a sentence, without a number in it?
