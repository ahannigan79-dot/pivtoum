# The short answer

**Not for document translation. Yes for interpreting, and for translation where someone is
legally accountable for accuracy.**

General document translation scores **8.8** out of 10 — ==-the highest score of any career
track in this index.== Court and legal interpreting scores **4.3**.

Same languages. Same training. ==A 4.5-point gap, which is the widest internal split we
measure anywhere.==

And the reason isn't quality. It's consequence.

# The number that explains everything

One statistic captures this entire profile, and it comes from industry research rather than
from us.

==+**73% of enterprises use AI translation internally. Only 19% rely on it for external or
regulated communication.**==

*(CSA Research)*

**That single split is the whole map.**

Internal documents, first drafts, comprehension, low-stakes bulk content — automated, at
enormous scale, and there is no route back. External communication, regulated content, anything
where an error has legal or clinical consequences — still human, and increasingly so.

**Now look at the economics, because they explain why the split is so absolute.**

| | Cost |
|---|---|
| Machine translation APIs | **$10–$20 per million characters** — fractions of a cent per word |
| Professional human translation | **$0.08–$0.50 per word** |
| Specialist legal/medical translation | **$0.15–$0.50 per word** |
| Live interpreting | **$80–$250 per hour** |
| Conference interpreting | **$600–$1,200 per day** |

==-That is a cost difference of two to three orders of magnitude.== No amount of quality
advantage survives a gap that size for routine content — which is exactly what the 73% figure
describes.

**So the survival condition isn't quality. It's consequence.**

Human translation persists where the cost of being wrong exceeds the cost of the human by a
wide margin: a mistranslated clinical instruction, a contract clause, a court testimony, an
immigration document. ==?In those settings the price differential becomes irrelevant, because
nobody is optimizing for translation cost when the downside is a misdiagnosis or a wrongful
conviction.==

**The general lesson, and it transfers well beyond languages:** when a task becomes nearly free
to automate, ask what remains of it. What remains is not the version that was done best — it's
the version where someone must be answerable for the result.

# Why interpreting holds and translation doesn't

The two are usually spoken of together and they are structurally different jobs.

**Translation is text in, text out.** Asynchronous, reviewable, correctable, and produced
without anyone present. Every one of those properties makes it automatable, and they are
properties of the medium rather than of the difficulty.

**Interpreting is a person in a room, in real time, under pressure.**

- **It's physical and present.** A court interpreter is in the courtroom; a medical interpreter
  is at the bedside. ==+Presence isn't incidental to the work — in many settings it's legally
  required.==
- **It's real-time and irreversible.** There is no draft, no review, no second pass. The
  sentence is said and its consequences begin immediately.
- **It carries personal accountability.** A certified court interpreter is answerable for
  accuracy in a way no system can be, and the certification attaches to a named individual.
- **It handles what text doesn't contain** — tone, hesitation, register, the witness who is
  frightened, the patient who is minimizing symptoms.

==+That's why court interpreting scores 4.3 and document translation 8.8 with identical
language skills. The languages are the same. Everything structural about the work is
different.==

# What the trend shows

**General document translation: 7.7 → 8.4 → 8.8.** Up 1.1 — from a base that was already the
highest in the index.

**Court and legal interpreting: 3.6 → 4.0 → 4.3.** Up 0.7.

**The gap widened from 4.1 points to 4.5.** What is distinctive here is the *timing*: most
careers in this index show a step change after 2023. ==-Translation was already substantially
displaced by then, because text-to-text conversion was the first thing these systems did well.
It is the most mature displacement we measure, and it is the closest thing we have to seeing
where other professions end up.==

# How durable is the protection?

| Protection | Strength today | Durability | Where the pressure is coming from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Human trust / accountability** | Strong for interpreting | **Most durable** | Certification attaches to a named person answerable in court. |
| **Regulatory / licensing** | Real and strengthening | **Durable, and expanding** | Language-access law is broadening rather than narrowing. |
| **Physical / embodied** | Real for interpreting | **Durable** | Presence is legally required in many settings. |
| **Judgment under novelty** | Moderate | **Eroding** | Register and nuance resist; standard content does not. |

**The honest read.** ==+Interpreting's protections are genuine and mutually reinforcing —
presence, accountability and law. Translation's are almost entirely absent, which is why the
score is what it is.==

And one protection here is *growing*: compliance obligations around language access —
Title VI requirements, healthcare access rules, court accuracy mandates — are expanding, and
each one creates demand for accountable human language work.

# How the role will actually change

| Was the job | Is becoming the job |
|---|---|
| Translating the document | Post-editing machine output — a different and lower-paid job |
| Producing first drafts | Reviewing generated drafts |
| Bulk and repetitive content | Gone, at any professional rate |
| Terminology research | Automated |
| Interpreting in a courtroom | **Unchanged** |
| Being certified and accountable | **Unchanged** |

**One development deserves naming honestly.** ==-Machine translation post-editing has become a
substantial share of the translation market, and it pays materially less than translation
did.== It is real work and it is a real career for some people. It is also the clearest example
in this index of a profession's core activity being converted into a checking function — the
thing we describe as a risk elsewhere, already happened here.

# Human strengths that will matter most

1. **Real-time interpreting under pressure** — no draft, no review, immediate consequences. The
   least automatable thing in this profession.
2. **Certified accountability** — being the named person answerable for accuracy in a legal or
   clinical setting.
3. **Domain expertise** — law, medicine, engineering. ==+The differentiator is increasingly the
   subject knowledge rather than the language.==
4. **Cultural and register judgment** — knowing what a phrase means to *this* audience, which
   is the whole of transcreation and much of literary work.
5. **Reading the person** — the hesitation, the evasion, the distress that the words don't
   carry.

Notably absent: speed, volume and breadth of general vocabulary. These were the traditional
markers of a strong translator and are the fastest-depreciating.

# Other things to consider about this job

## What's genuinely good about it

**The protected end is genuinely well paid and in demand.**

- BLS projects roughly **6,900 new interpreter and translator openings a year** through 2034
- ==+Live interpreting runs **$80–$250 per hour**; conference interpreting **$600–$1,200 per
  day**; legal interpreting **$350–$750 per day**==
- Specialist translators (legal, medical, technical) bill **$60–$150 per hour** and have
  **maintained or increased** their rates while generalists face downward pressure
- Prospects are strongest for Portuguese, Mandarin, Korean, Arabic and Spanish — and
  ==+"particularly strong for interpreters who combine language fluency with domain expertise
  in law, medicine or technology"==

**Compliance is a growing tailwind.** Language-access requirements in healthcare, courts,
education and government are expanding, and each one mandates accountable human provision.

**And in-house roles offer stability** — government agencies, hospitals and large law firms
employ interpreters on salary with benefits and predictable workloads.

**What practitioners report as rewarding:** being the reason two people can understand each
other at a moment that matters; genuine intellectual engagement with language and subject
matter; and unusual autonomy for those who freelance well.

## What's genuinely hard about it

**The generalist market has collapsed and is not coming back.** ==-Rates at the floor of the
market have reached $0.05 per word, and machine translation costs fractions of a cent.== A
career built on general translation competence has no economic foundation left.

**Freelancing is the default**, with all that implies: income variability, no benefits, and the
business of finding clients being a second job.

**Interpreting is demanding in ways people underestimate** — sustained concentration,
emotionally difficult content in medical and legal settings, and no opportunity to correct a
mistake.

**And certification is essential but fragmented.** In the US there is no single government
certifying body for translators; court and medical interpreting have their own certification
regimes, and requirements vary by state.

## Who this work suits — and who it doesn't

Translation and interpreting tend to suit people who:

- **Love languages *and* something else.** ==?The single most important trait now. Language
  alone is no longer a career; language plus law, medicine or engineering is.==
- **Can hold concentration under pressure** — interpreting is cognitively brutal in bursts
- **Are comfortable with high-stakes accuracy**, where an error has consequences
- **Are self-directed**, because most of this market is freelance
- **Are genuinely interested in people**, not only in text

It's harder for people who:

- Want **salaried stability** — it exists but it is a minority of the market
- Are drawn primarily to **written translation** as a craft. That's the exposed end.
- Need **predictable income** early
- Assume that **fluency is the qualification.** It is the entry ticket.

## What else is moving this market

**Language-access regulation is expanding**, and it is the most reliable source of demand for
accountable human language work.

**Domain specialization is the whole game.** The rate data is unambiguous: generalists face
downward pressure, specialists in legal, medical and creative fields have held or increased
rates.

**And in-house demand is growing in regulated sectors** — healthcare systems, courts and
government agencies increasingly employ rather than contract, because accountability is easier
to manage.

# The AI-native advantage — what to actually do about it

**The situation:** this profession is further through the transition than any other in this
index. The strategic question isn't whether AI will affect it — that's settled — but which
version of the work someone aims at.

### What an AI-native linguist looks like

**1. Aim at accountable work from the start.** ==+Court, medical, immigration, regulated
content. Where an error has consequences, the price differential stops mattering.==

**2. Get certified, specifically.** Court interpreting and medical interpreting certifications
are the credential that converts language ability into protected work.

**3. Build genuine domain knowledge.** Legal, medical or technical. This is now the
differentiator, and the rate data proves it.

**4. Understand post-editing honestly.** It is a real market and it pays less than translation
did. Knowing that going in is better than discovering it.

**5. Learn to direct and verify.** For the content that remains human-supervised, the skill is
knowing where machine output fails — false fluency, register errors, terminology that is
plausible and wrong.

### Concrete preparation

**Before the degree:** ==+decide whether the goal is interpreting or translation, because they
are different careers with a 4.5-point exposure gap.== If it's interpreting, that shapes the
whole path.

**During:** pair languages with a substantive second subject rather than treating language as
the whole degree. Get interpreting practice specifically — it's a distinct skill from
translation and it is trained differently.

**After:** pursue certification early, and target regulated sectors.

# Where language skills can lead later

Interpreting across legal, medical, conference and community settings; specialist and certified
translation; localization and transcreation; language-access coordination; international
policy, diplomacy and development; language teaching; and any commercial role where the
language is paired with genuine domain expertise.

**The pattern in this index holds with unusual force:** ==?the further the work sits from a
person in a room and an accountable signature, the higher the exposure.==

---

# What to look for in a program

**1. Is there genuine interpreting training?**
Not translation with an interpreting module — actual interpreting practice, which is a distinct
skill.

**2. Does it support certification?**
Court and medical interpreting certification is what converts the degree into protected work.

**3. Can students pair the language with a real second subject?**
This is now the differentiator and many language programs still don't facilitate it.

**4. Does the curriculum engage honestly with machine translation and post-editing?**
A program that ignores it is preparing students for a market that no longer exists.

**5. Graduate destinations by type** — interpreting, specialist translation, post-editing, or
outside the field entirely.

---

# Questions to ask a program

- How much of the program is interpreting practice rather than translation?
- Does the program prepare students for court or medical interpreting certification?
- Can students combine the language with law, medicine, engineering or another substantive
  subject?
- How does the curriculum address machine translation and post-editing?
- Where are graduates working at two years — interpreting, specialist translation, post-editing,
  or elsewhere?

**Red flags:** a curriculum built around general translation practice; no interpreting stream;
no certification pathway; no engagement with machine translation; outcomes described as
"working with languages."

- **[Translation rates and pricing, 2026](https://www.biztoolkit.co/post/translation-localization-rates-in-2026-per-word-per-hour-per-project)**

**The counterargument — read this one:**

  The strongest case against our 8.8 is that we are scoring a *job title* that has already
  changed rather than the work that replaced it.

  On this reading, translation didn't disappear — it became post-editing, quality assurance and
  terminology management, and the volume of content requiring language work has grown enormously
  as machine translation made multilingual content viable for organisations that could never
  previously afford it. ==?More content is being translated than at any point in history, and a
  human is still involved in most of what matters.==

  There's real support for it: the 73%/19% split shows enterprises using AI internally while
  keeping humans on anything external, and compliance-driven demand is growing.

  **Where we land:** we think this describes a genuine phenomenon and understates what it costs
  the practitioner. Post-editing is real work at materially lower rates than translation
  commanded — the volume grew and the value per unit collapsed. ==+A profession where the work
  survives at a third of the price is not the same as a profession that survived.==

  **What we're watching:** whether specialist and certified rates hold. They have so far, and
  they are the thing that makes the protected end viable. If they start falling, this profile
  gets worse rather than better.

# Bottom line

**Translation contains the most exposed track in this index and a genuinely protected career,
and they share a qualification.**

The reason is captured in one statistic: ==**73% of enterprises use AI translation internally;
only 19% rely on it for external or regulated communication.**== Everything low-stakes has gone,
at a cost difference of two to three orders of magnitude. Everything consequential has stayed.

**So the survival condition is not quality — it's consequence.** Human language work persists
where being wrong has legal or clinical cost, and nowhere else.

**Which makes the practical advice unusually clear.** ==Aim at interpreting rather than
translation.== Get court or medical certification. Pair the language with law, medicine or
engineering, because the rate data shows specialists holding rates while generalists lose
theirs. And understand that a general translation career has no economic floor left.

And one honest thing worth saying to a seventeen-year-old who loves languages. ==+Nothing here
says that's the wrong instinct. It says fluency has stopped being the qualification and become
the entry ticket — and that being the person in the room, accountable, when two people
absolutely must understand each other, is work that is not going anywhere.==

# Discussion questions

## Part 1 — About the work itself

1. **Do they want to translate, or to interpret?** These are different careers with a 4.5-point
   exposure gap, and most people haven't distinguished them.
2. What made languages appealing — the language itself, or communicating between people?
3. Can they hold concentration under sustained pressure? Interpreting is cognitively brutal.
4. How would they handle emotionally difficult content — a medical consultation, a court
   testimony?

## Part 2 — Reacting to the findings

5. Document translation scores 8.8 and court interpreting 4.3. Was that surprising?
6. **73% of enterprises use AI translation internally; only 19% for regulated communication.**
   What does that suggest about where to aim?
7. The profile argues the survival condition is consequence rather than quality. Does that
   land — and does it apply to other careers they're considering?

## Part 2b — The harder conversation

8. Machine translation costs fractions of a cent per word against $0.08–$0.50 for human work.
   How do they think about competing with that?
9. Post-editing is a real market that pays less than translation did. Is that acceptable as a
   fallback?
10. Most of this market is freelance. Have you discussed what that means financially?

## Part 2c — The other side

11. Court interpreting pays $350–$750 a day and conference work $600–$1,200. Does the protected
    end look different from the headline score?
12. Of what practitioners find rewarding — enabling understanding at moments that matter,
    intellectual engagement, autonomy — which matters most?
13. Looking at "who this work suits": which items sound like them? Answer separately, then
    compare.

## Part 2d — The AI question, practically

14. The differentiator is now the *domain* rather than the language. What second subject would
    genuinely interest them?
15. Machine output fails through false fluency — plausible and wrong. What does that suggest
    about verification as a skill?

## Part 3 — Testing it against reality

16. **The most valuable single action:** observe a court or medical interpreter working, if
    access can be arranged. It's a completely different job from what most people picture.
17. Find someone working in languages and ask what proportion of their income is translation,
    interpreting and post-editing.

## Part 4 — About the program

18. Ask each program the interpreting and certification questions from section 13.
19. Can they pair the language with a substantive second subject?

## Part 5 — Widening the frame

20. Have they considered **languages plus law, medicine or engineering** as a combined path?
21. What about **in-house roles** in health systems, courts or government? Salaried, stable, and
    growing with compliance requirements.
22. If languages weren't available as a career, what would they choose? The answer often reveals
    whether the pull is language or communication.

## Part 6 — For the parent, alone

23. Am I about to tell them languages aren't a career? Because the interpreting data says
    otherwise.
24. Can I help them steer toward the protected end rather than away from the field?
25. What's the one thing I most want them to understand — in a sentence, without a number in it?
