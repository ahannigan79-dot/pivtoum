# So you're thinking about accounting

*Written for you, not about you.*

---

We scored twenty-seven careers on how exposed they are to AI.

A licensed CPA doing audit and advisory: **5.2** out of 10.

Bookkeeping and transactional accounting: **7.9**.

==Same profession. The licence is doing nearly all of the work.==

---

## The 60-second version

- The licensed end holds up. The tier below it isn't really a career any more.
- ==-Bookkeeping was already at 6.8 back in 2023== — before generative AI existed.
- There's a severe shortage: **120,000+ openings a year against about 55,000 graduates.**
- ==+43 states have changed the rules to make qualifying faster and cheaper.== That's new.
- The honest weakness is the starting salary, and it's a real gap.

---

## The thing that just changed

Start here, because it's the most useful thing in this document and it's about a year old.

For forty years, becoming a CPA required **150 credit hours** — basically a fifth year of
university. It cost an extra year of tuition plus a year of not earning, and it became the
single biggest reason people picked finance or tech instead.

The profession noticed. Enrolments fell for over a decade. CPA exam numbers hit their lowest
since 2006. Around 300,000 accountants left in five years.

**So they changed it.** Roughly **43 states** have now altered or scrapped the 150-hour rule.
The common new route is a normal bachelor's degree plus two years of supervised experience,
instead of a fifth year of study.

==+A profession deliberately making it *easier* to get in, while most professions are making
it harder, is genuinely unusual.==

**Practical instruction: check the current rules wherever you'd actually qualify.** Anything
your school or a careers site told you before 2025 is probably out of date.

---

## Why the licensed end is protected

**Only a licensed CPA can sign an audit opinion.** Not review it, not supervise it — sign it,
with personal liability attached. ==+Liability is a legal thing, not a technical thing. No
amount of capability transfers it.==

**And here's the part that matters more for you.** The CPA pathway also *requires supervised
experience hours* — which means firms have to keep hiring and training juniors, even where the
work could be automated.

Compare law. A bar card protects the courtroom and the signature. It says nothing about who
reads forty thousand documents, so that work went — and junior law now scores 7.9.

==?So when you look at any career with a licence, ask what it actually reserves — and whether
it reserves the training too.== That second one matters far more when you're the one trying to
get in.

## What already happened, and what it tells you

Most careers in this index show a sharp change after 2023. Accounting doesn't — because its
bottom tier was automated years earlier.

Data entry, reconciliation, categorisation: these were among the very first white-collar tasks
to go, and that was mature before generative AI arrived.

==-So accounting is the closest thing we have to seeing what "finished" looks like. Not
collapse — a tier that quietly stopped being a career while the profession above it carried
on.==

---

## The shortage, and the trap in reading it

The numbers are real:

- **120,000+ annual openings** projected by BLS, against roughly **55,000 graduates**
- Around **190,000–200,000 open positions** at any time
- **300,000 accountants left in five years**, and those remaining skew late-career
- Starting salaries rising in response

**But here's the trap.** ==?The shortage is severe *and* the entry tier is automating, at the
same time.== Both are true, because they're happening at different levels. The profession
badly needs licensed CPAs and barely needs bookkeepers.

If you read "accounting shortage" as a general green light, you've misread it. It's a green
light for the licensed path specifically.

## What we won't soften

**The starting salary gap is the profession's real problem.**

- First-year audit associate: **$55,000–$70,000**
- First-year financial analyst: **$75,000–$95,000**
- First-year software engineer: **$90,000–$130,000**

==-Same students. Same universities. And the gap has widened every single year.==

That is the honest reason people pick finance or tech instead, and no amount of talking about
job security makes those numbers different.

**Busy season is real too.** January to April in tax and audit is genuinely punishing, every
year, and it's the most-cited reason people leave for industry.

What you get in exchange: a licence, a severe shortage, and a door that's currently getting
wider. Whether that trade is worth it is a real question, not a rhetorical one.

## Does this actually sound like you?

Accounting suits people who:

- Are **comfortable in detail** — not tolerant of it, comfortable in it. Different things.
- Like **understanding how businesses actually work** from the inside
- Are **sceptical by nature.** Professional scepticism — not taking things at face value — is
  the core skill, and it's closer to temperament than training.
- Want a **clear qualification and a marked path**, which accounting gives you better than
  almost any other business route

Harder if you want high early earnings, need variety over rhythm, or find detail draining
rather than absorbing.

## The skill nobody's teaching yet

**Audit is changing more than the score suggests**, and in a way that's actually more
interesting.

It used to mean testing a *sample*. Now you can test the *whole population* automatically —
which means the job becomes investigating the exceptions the system flagged, rather than
checking a selection by hand.

That's a better job. It's also a different skill from the one accountancy training has
historically built.

**And the thing to protect:** ==+accountants used to learn a client's business by building its
numbers. That was boring and it was the education.== Now it's automated, so understanding the
business has to be pursued deliberately — and that understanding is what separates someone who
can sign from someone who can only prepare.

---

## What actually matters when picking a course

- **Does it map to the *current* CPA requirements** where you'd qualify? Ask directly — this
  has changed.
- **Internship placement and conversion rate.** The profession recruits through internships,
  and the shortage means access is easier than it's been in years.
- **How much compulsory audit and assurance content?** That's where the protection lives. A
  course light on audit is training you for the exposed tier.
- **Do they teach data analytics and audit technology?**

---

## Two things worth doing

**Get an internship.** Firms are short-staffed and recruiting hard, so this is more accessible
right now than it has been in a long time. It's also how most people get hired.

**Then find someone three to five years into public practice** and ask what they actually did
in year one — and whether that work still exists in the same form.

---

## One route note

==+Go into public practice first, then industry if you want.== The reverse rarely works, and
public practice is where the supervised hours and the licence come from.

That's the single most common mistake people make with this career.
