## Before you read this — a note for parents

You're likely reading this before your child is. That's normal, and it's worth pausing on how
you use it.

**Don't lead with the scores.** If you open with a risk number, they either get frightened and
shut down, or defensive about a decision they've already made.

**What this is.** A facts document, not a recommendation.

**A practical note:** there's a separate short version written directly to them. Send them
that one.

**And one thing specific to dentistry.** ==?This is the profession where our whole framework
has already been tested by history — and it passed.== A tier of dentistry was automated more
than a decade ago, before anyone was discussing AI, and the profession is fine. Section 4
explains what happened and what it predicts.

The honest issues here are financial, and they are substantial. Section 6 is the one to read
carefully.

# The short answer

**Yes, and dentistry is among the best-protected work we score.**

General dentistry scores **2.7** out of 10. Oral surgery scores **2.3**. Dental hygiene scores
**3.0**.

==+Physical work in a patient's mouth, comprehensive licensure, and a relationship built over
years — that combination is close to the strongest in this index.==

The reasons to think hard about dentistry are entirely financial: **average dental school debt
of $293,900**, and an income picture that depends heavily on whether someone eventually owns a
practice.

# Why dentistry scores so low — the six factors

Here's how general dentistry answers them.

### How much of this job can AI already do? *(35% of the score)*

**Imaging support, scheduling, records, and treatment planning assistance. Not the dentistry.**

Radiograph analysis and caries detection. Treatment plan drafting. Clinical notes and records.
Appointment scheduling and recall. Insurance claims and coding. ==+Restorative design and
milling — which section 4 covers, because that one already happened.==

What resists: working inside a mouth with hand instruments, managing a frightened patient,
judgment about what a specific tooth needs, and the relationship that brings someone back for
twenty years.

### Is the path in and up being eroded? *(15%)*

**Strong protection.** Licensure requires supervised clinical training and school places are
capped, so entry is competitive but employment afterwards is secure.

### Does it have to be done in person, with your hands? *(15%)*

**Almost entirely — and with unusual precision.** Dentistry is fine motor work in a confined,
wet, moving space, on a patient who may be anxious or in pain. This is among the harder
physical problems in medicine, not among the easier ones.

### Does someone need a human they can trust and hold responsible? *(15%)*

**Yes, and unusually so.** Dentistry involves fear more than most healthcare, and trust is what
gets people through the door. A licensed dentist is professionally answerable for the outcome.

### Does the law require a licensed human? *(10%)*

**Comprehensively.** Practice, prescribing and procedures are all legally reserved.

### How often does the job hit genuinely new, high-stakes situations? *(10%)*

**Regularly.** Every mouth is different, and the judgment about restoration, extraction or
referral is made with incomplete information about what's under the surface.

# Dentistry already ran this experiment

This is the most useful section in the profile, and it applies well beyond dentistry.

**Dental laboratory technology was automated more than a decade before the AI conversation
started.**

For most of the twentieth century, crowns, bridges and dentures were made by hand by skilled
technicians — a genuine craft, learned over years, requiring real artistry. Then CAD/CAM
arrived: digital scanning, computer design, milling machines. ==-Much of that work moved from a
bench to a screen and a machine, and the number of people needed to do it fell substantially.==

**Notice what happened next, because it is exactly what our framework predicts.**

**The profession was fine.** Dentists still practice. Patients still get crowns. Demand didn't
fall.

**But a whole tier of skilled, well-regarded craft work contracted permanently**, and the people
in it did not simply move up — many moved out. Dental lab technicians now report median pay
around $58,307, and the work that remains is increasingly digital design rather than manual
fabrication.

**And that is why lab technology still scores 5.5 today** while chairside dentistry scores 2.7.
The exposure isn't in front of it. Much of it has already happened.

==?The general lesson, and it is the single most transferable idea in this index: automation
does not usually destroy a profession. It removes a *tier* of it — and which tier you are in
matters more than which profession you choose.==

**Three practical consequences worth carrying to any career:**

The tier that goes first is the one with **structured inputs and a physical output that a
machine can produce** — a crown from a scan, a document from a template, an image from a brief.

The tier that survives is the one **with the patient, the client, or the accountability** in
front of it.

And ==+the displacement is usually invisible from outside the profession. Nobody outside
dentistry noticed the lab tier contract.== The same is now happening inside law, accounting and
design, and it looks the same from the outside: the profession appears healthy, and one layer
of it has quietly stopped being a career.

# What the trend shows

**General dentistry: 2.3 → 2.5 → 2.7.** Up 0.4 in three years, and all of it administrative.

**Orthodontics: 3.0 → 3.4 → 3.8.** Up 0.8 — the fastest-moving track in the profession, driven
by treatment planning, aligner design and remote monitoring being genuinely automatable.

**Dental lab: 4.8 → 5.2 → 5.5.** Continuing a displacement that started years ago.

**The gap widened from 2.8 points to 3.2.** The pattern holds exactly: ==the further the work
moves from the patient's mouth, the higher the exposure climbs.==

# The financial picture — and this is the real decision

Dentistry is one of the most expensive qualifications available, and the numbers deserve
careful reading.

**The debt:**

- ==-Average dental school debt is **$293,900**, per the ADA's Survey of Dental School
  Seniors.==
- The spread is enormous: public schools typically **$200,000–$260,000**, private around
  **$321,000**, and the most expensive programs reach **$625,000–$700,000**.
- One institution's program cost alone is reported at **$492,820**.

**The income, and the gap inside it:**

- BLS reports a mean annual wage of **$166,810** for general dentists.
- ==+ADA Health Policy Institute puts median net income for practice *owners* closer to
  **$239,000**.==
- ADA HPI analysis of Department of Education data puts average net income for general
  practitioners **five years after graduation at $171,062**.
- New dentists typically earn **$150,000–$200,000** in their first year.
- Specialists out-earn generalists by roughly 30–90%.

==?That $166,810-versus-$239,000 gap is the whole story. Dentistry's income model assumes
eventual practice ownership, and a student should understand that at seventeen rather than at
thirty-two.==

**And one statistic that says something the others don't.** ADA data indicates that around
**35% of dental school costs are financed without education loans**, with a further 17% covered
by family. ==?Dentistry draws disproportionately from families able to fund it, which means the
"average debt" figure understates the burden for students who borrow the whole amount.==

**The practical instruction is the same as for physical therapy:** the spread between the
cheapest and most expensive accredited program is larger than most people's mortgages.
==+Attend the least expensive accredited school you get into.==

# The alternative that deserves proper attention

**Dental hygiene, and the numbers are genuinely striking.**

- ==+Median pay of **$98,100** (BLS OEWS, May 2025) — slightly *above* the registered nurse
  median of $97,550==
- **7% projected growth** through 2034, with about **15,300 openings a year**
- Around **222,740** practicing hygienists, 94% of them in dentists' offices
- **No night or weekend shifts**, unlike nursing
- ==+Program debt typically **$30,000–$80,000** — an order of magnitude below dental school==
- And it scores **3.0** on our index, against 2.7 for dentistry

**Two honest caveats**, because this isn't a free lunch:

==-Part-time work is the norm rather than the exception. In ADA Health Policy Institute's survey
of 5,122 hygienists, 28.8% worked under 32 hours a week against 53.7% full-time== — roughly one
in three part-time. Annual salary figures assume full-time, year-round hours that many
hygienists don't work.

**And benefits follow from that.** BLS notes that vacation, sick leave and retirement
contributions may only be available to full-time staff, and ADA research finds health insurance
and paid leave are far from universal in private dental practices.

**There is also less of a ladder.** Nursing offers a longer advancement path; hygiene is
relatively flat.

==?But for someone drawn to clinical dental work rather than to diagnosis, business ownership
and treatment planning — two to three years and $50,000 of debt against seven-plus years and
$293,900 is a comparison worth making properly.==

# How durable is the protection?

| Protection | Strength today | Durability | Where the pressure is coming from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Physical / embodied** | Very strong | **Most fragile long-term** | The protection physical AI targets — though a wet, confined, moving mouth is among the harder robotics problems. |
| **Regulatory / licensing** | Strong | **Most durable** | Practice and procedures are legally reserved. |
| **Human trust / accountability** | Strong | **Durable** | Dentistry involves fear; trust is what brings people back. |
| **Judgment under novelty** | Good | **Eroding slowly** | Diagnostic imaging support is advancing; chairside judgment is not. |

**The honest read.** ==+Dentistry has all four protections operating together, which is rare —
and the historical evidence in section 4 shows the framework working prospectively.== The
automation arrived where the model says it should: at the structured, machine-producible tier.

# How the role will actually change

| Was the job | Is becoming the job |
|---|---|
| Reading the radiograph unaided | First-pass caries detection arrives; the dentist adjudicates |
| Writing clinical notes | Reviewing an ambient draft |
| Fabricating restorations by hand | ==-Already gone — CAD/CAM did this a decade ago== |
| Drafting the treatment plan | Reviewing a generated plan and judging whether it fits |
| Insurance coding and claims | Substantially automated |
| Working inside the mouth | **Unchanged** |
| Managing a frightened patient | **Unchanged** |

# Human strengths that will matter most

1. **Fine motor work in a confined space** — the core of the craft, and among the hardest
   physical problems in medicine.
2. **Managing fear** — dentistry involves more patient anxiety than most healthcare, and
   handling it well is a genuine and undersupplied skill.
3. **Judgment on what a specific tooth needs** — restore, extract, refer, or wait.
4. **Long-term relationships** — the practice model depends on people coming back for decades.
5. **Verification** — knowing when an automated caries detection or treatment plan is wrong.

# Other things to consider about this job

## What's genuinely good about it

**Satisfaction is high and unusually consistent.** Survey data puts career satisfaction around
**74%**, with **85% saying they would choose their specialty again** — figures at the higher end
of healthcare.

**And the working pattern is better than most of medicine.** Dentists report averaging around
**36 hours a week**, with no routine night or weekend work in most general practice — a genuine
difference from medicine and nursing.

**What dentists report as rewarding:**

- **Immediate, visible results** — you fix something and see it fixed
- **Combining manual craft with clinical judgment**, which few careers offer
- **Autonomy**, with practice ownership a normal destination rather than an exotic one
- **Long relationships** with patients and families across decades
- **Genuine control over working pattern**, particularly for owners

## What's genuinely hard about it

**The debt, covered in section 6, and it is the dominant issue.**

**The physical toll is real and under-discussed.** Dentistry is performed in sustained awkward
postures, and musculoskeletal problems — neck, back, shoulders, wrists — are a recognized
occupational hazard that can shorten careers.

**Patient anxiety is a constant.** A meaningful share of patients arrive frightened, and
managing that repeatedly is emotionally demanding in a way outsiders rarely appreciate.

**The business is a second job.** ==-Ownership is where the income is, and running a practice
means staff, overheads, insurance negotiation and marketing — none of which dental school
teaches well.==

**And insurance economics are a persistent frustration**, with reimbursement pressure a leading
complaint in the profession.

## Who this work suits — and who it doesn't

Dentistry tends to suit people who:

- **Have genuine manual dexterity** and enjoy precise physical work
- **Are comfortable with people who are frightened** — this is most of the interpersonal job
- **Want immediate, visible results** rather than long-horizon outcomes
- **Are interested in running a business**, or at least willing to be
- **Can work in confined, repetitive physical conditions** for decades

It's harder for people who:

- Want **variety of setting** — it's largely the same room, the same chair
- Are uncomfortable **causing discomfort**, which is unavoidable
- Have no interest in the **commercial side**, given where the income sits
- Need **intellectual novelty** daily — much of general practice is repetitive by design

## What else is moving this market

**Consolidation is reshaping the profession.** Dental support organisations have grown
substantially, and the proportion of dentists in traditional solo private practice has fallen.
==?That changes the ownership assumption underpinning the income model, and it's the most
important structural shift in the profession right now.==

**Demand is stable and demographically supported**, with an ageing population retaining more
natural teeth than previous generations.

**And the specialty premium is significant** — specialists out-earn generalists by 30–90%, at
the cost of additional years and additional debt.

# The AI-native advantage — what to actually do about it

**The situation:** dental AI is arriving as imaging support, treatment planning assistance and
administrative relief. The clinical work is untouched.

### What an AI-native dentist looks like

**1. Verify.** Knowing when an automated caries detection is wrong in either direction. ==+A
false positive means unnecessary treatment on a healthy tooth, which is a clinical and ethical
problem, not just a technical one.==

**2. Use imaging support properly.** These systems are genuinely good and genuinely confident
when wrong.

**3. Run the digital workflow.** Intraoral scanning, CAD/CAM chairside milling, digital
treatment planning. ==+The lab tier moved into the practice — a dentist who can operate that
workflow captures value that used to leave the building.==

**4. Handle patient AI use.** Patients arrive having consulted symptom checkers and comparison
sites, often about cost. Working with that rather than dismissing it is a new communication
skill.

**5. Take the business seriously.** Everything in the financial section points at ownership, and
practice management is the least-taught and most consequential skill in the profession.

### Concrete preparation

**Before applying:** ==+compare total program cost seriously. The spread between the cheapest
and most expensive accredited school exceeds $400,000, for the same license.==

**During:** take practice management and business content seriously rather than treating it as
peripheral. Get exposure to digital workflows, and to a range of practice settings including
DSO-affiliated ones.

**Entering:** understand associate versus ownership economics before signing anything, and get
any employment contract reviewed.

# Where a dental qualification can lead later

Clinical practice across general and specialist areas, practice ownership and multi-site
groups, academia and teaching, public and community dental health, industry and device
companies, and dental research.

**The pattern in this index holds:** ==?the further from the chair, the higher the exposure.
Laboratory and administrative roles score 5.5 and above against chairside practice at 2.7.==

---

# What to look for in a dental program

**1. Total cost — and calculate the debt-to-income ratio yourself.**
This matters more than anything else on this list.

**2. Accreditation**, which is non-negotiable.

**3. Clinical volume — how many procedures does a student actually perform?**
This varies enormously between schools and it determines competence at graduation.

**4. Digital workflow and practice management content.**
Both are where the profession is going and both are commonly underweighted.

**5. Board pass rates and first-destination outcomes.**

---

# Questions to ask a dental program

**On cost — ask these first:**
- What is the total cost of attendance for the full program, including living expenses?
- What is the median debt at graduation for your graduates?

**On clinical training:**
- How many procedures does a typical student complete before graduating?
- What range of settings do students see, including community and DSO practices?

**On the curriculum:**
- How much practice management and business content is compulsory?
- How does the program teach digital workflow — scanning, CAD/CAM, treatment planning?
- Where do students learn to verify AI-assisted imaging analysis?

**Red flags:** evasiveness about total cost or graduate debt; low clinical procedure volume;
practice management treated as optional; no digital workflow training.

- **[Dentists — Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/dentists.htm)**
  — *US Bureau of Labor Statistics.* Median pay, projected growth and entry requirements. Read

  available on this decision.

- **[Average dental school debt](https://educationdata.org/average-dental-school-debt)**
  — program-by-program cost data, including the finding that around 35% of dental school
  costs are financed without loans. Worth reading before choosing where to apply.

**The counterargument — read this one:**

  The strongest case against our 2.7 isn't that AI will replace dentists. ==?It's that dentistry
  already showed us what happens, and we may be under-reading it.==

  On this view, the lab tier's disappearance is not reassurance — it is the warning. A skilled,
  respected, decades-old craft inside dentistry was substantially automated within about ten
  years of the enabling technology maturing, and the people in it did not move up. If milling
  machines could do that to lab work, the argument runs, chairside robotics could eventually do
  something similar to routine restorative procedures, and our 2.7 reflects present capability
  rather than trajectory.

  **Where we land:** we take it seriously, and it's why physical protection is the factor we
  rank least durable everywhere in this index. What holds our score is the specific difficulty
  of the environment — a wet, confined, moving mouth belonging to an anxious person is a
  materially harder robotics problem than a milling machine working from a scan, and the
  regulatory and trust protections would remain even if the manual problem were solved.

  ==?But the honest position is that dentistry demonstrates our framework works — including
  when the answer is uncomfortable.==

# Bottom line

**On AI exposure, dentistry is among the safest work we measure.** Chairside practice at 2.7 is
protected by all four of our factors at once — fine motor work in a mouth, comprehensive
licensure, deep patient trust, and clinical judgment on incomplete information.

**And dentistry gives this whole index its clearest proof.** ==The lab tier was automated more
than a decade before anyone discussed AI, the profession was fine, and a tier of skilled craft
work contracted permanently. Automation removes a tier, not a profession — and which tier you're
in matters more than which profession you choose.==

**The real decision is financial.** Average debt of **$293,900**, with a spread between schools
exceeding $400,000 for the identical license — against a mean wage of $166,810 that rises to
around $239,000 for practice owners. ==The income model assumes ownership, and that assumption
is worth understanding at seventeen rather than at thirty-two.==

**So the useful advice is:** attend the least expensive accredited school you get into, because
the spread dwarfs everything else. Take practice management seriously, since ownership is where
the income sits. Learn the digital workflow, because the lab tier moved into the practice and
whoever runs it captures the value.

And look properly at **dental hygiene** before committing — median pay slightly above a
registered nurse, no nights or weekends, debt an order of magnitude lower, and a score of 3.0.
==+With the honest caveat that a third of hygienists work part-time and benefits are patchy —
but for someone drawn to clinical dental work rather than to business ownership, that comparison
deserves an hour rather than a dismissal.==

# Discussion questions

## Part 1 — About the work itself

1. What made dentistry appealing? Push past "I want to work in healthcare" to something specific.
2. **Do they actually enjoy precise manual work?** This is the core of the job and it's testable.
3. Are they comfortable with people who are frightened, repeatedly?
4. Do they realize most general practice is the same room, the same chair, and largely repetitive
   by design?

## Part 2 — Reacting to the findings

5. Chairside dentistry scores 2.7 and lab technology 5.5. Does the difference make sense to them?
6. **The lab tier was automated a decade before the AI conversation.** What does that suggest
   about other careers they're considering?
7. Orthodontics is the fastest-moving track in the profession. Does that affect specialty
   thinking?

## Part 2b — The money conversation, and this is the important one

8. **Have you compared total cost across the schools they're considering?** Average debt is
   $293,900 and the spread between programs exceeds $400,000.
9. Do they understand that the income model assumes eventual practice ownership — $166,810
   employed against roughly $239,000 for owners?
10. Are they interested in running a business? Because that's where the money is, and dental
    school teaches it poorly.
11. **Have you looked at dental hygiene together?** $98,100 median, $30K–$80K debt, no nights or
    weekends — with the part-time and benefits caveats understood.

## Part 2c — The other side

12. Of what dentists find rewarding — visible results, manual craft with clinical judgment,
    autonomy, long relationships, a 36-hour week — which two matter most?
13. Looking at "who this work suits": which items sound like them? Answer separately, then
    compare.
14. Satisfaction sits around 74% and 85% would choose their specialty again. Does that surprise
    them?

## Part 2d — The AI question, practically

15. The lab work moved into the practice. Does running a digital workflow interest them?
16. Automated caries detection can produce false positives — treating a healthy tooth. What does
    that suggest about verification as a skill?

## Part 3 — Testing it against reality

17. **The most valuable single action:** shadow in two very different practices — a private
    owner-operated one and a DSO-affiliated one. The economics and the culture are genuinely
    different.
18. Find a dentist five years qualified and ask directly about their loans and whether they'd
    choose the same school.

## Part 4 — About the program

19. Ask each school the cost questions from section 14 — first, before anything else.
20. How many procedures does a student actually perform before graduating?
21. Is practice management compulsory?

## Part 5 — Widening the frame

22. Have they considered **dental hygiene or dental therapy** seriously, rather than as a
    fallback?
23. What about **medicine or physical therapy**? Both score similarly well with different
    financial profiles.
24. If dentistry weren't available, what would they choose? The answer usually reveals what
    they're drawn to.

## Part 6 — For the parent, alone

25. Am I comfortable with debt approaching $300,000, and have I said so clearly either way?
26. Would I support dental hygiene if it suited them better, or would I quietly see it as
    settling?
27. Do I understand that this career's income depends on them eventually running a business?
28. What's the one thing I most want them to understand — in a sentence, without a number in it?
