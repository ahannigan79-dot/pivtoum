# The short answer

**Not for document translation. Yes for interpreting, and for translation where someone is legally accountable for accuracy.**

General document translation scores **8.8** out of 10 — ==-the highest score of any career track in this index.== Court and legal interpreting scores **4.3**.

Same languages. Same training. ==A 4.5-point gap, which is the widest internal split we measure anywhere.==

And the reason isn't quality. It's consequence — and choosing which end of that gap you aim at is most of the decision in front of you.

# The number that explains everything

One statistic captures this entire guide, and it comes from industry research rather than from us.

==+**73% of enterprises use AI translation internally. Only 19% rely on it for external or regulated communication.**==

*(CSA Research)*

**That single split is the whole map.**

Internal documents, first drafts, comprehension, low-stakes bulk content — automated, at enormous scale, and there is no route back. External communication, regulated content, anything where an error has legal or clinical consequences — still human, and increasingly so.

**Now look at the economics, because they explain why the split is so absolute.**

| | Cost |
|---|---|
| Machine translation APIs | **$10–$20 per million characters** — fractions of a cent per word |
| Professional human translation | **$0.08–$0.50 per word** |
| Specialist legal/medical translation | **$0.15–$0.50 per word** |
| Live interpreting | **$80–$250 per hour** |
| Conference interpreting | **$600–$1,200 per day** |

==-That is a cost difference of two to three orders of magnitude.== No amount of quality advantage survives a gap that size for routine content — which is exactly what the 73% figure describes.

**So the survival condition isn't quality. It's consequence.**

Human translation persists where the cost of being wrong exceeds the cost of the human by a wide margin: a mistranslated clinical instruction, a contract clause, a court testimony, an immigration document. ==?In those settings the price differential becomes irrelevant, because nobody is optimizing for translation cost when the downside is a misdiagnosis or a wrongful conviction.==

**The general lesson, and it transfers well beyond languages:** when a task becomes nearly free to automate, ask what remains of it. What remains is not the version that was done best — it's the version where someone must be answerable for the result.

# Why interpreting holds and translation doesn't

The two are usually spoken of together and they are structurally different jobs.

**Translation is text in, text out.** Asynchronous, reviewable, correctable, and produced without anyone present. Every one of those properties makes it automatable, and they are properties of the medium rather than of the difficulty.

**Interpreting is a person in a room, in real time, under pressure.**

- **It's physical and present.** A court interpreter is in the courtroom; a medical interpreter is at the bedside. ==+Presence isn't incidental to the work — in many settings it's legally required.==
- **It's real-time and irreversible.** There is no draft, no review, no second pass. The sentence is said and its consequences begin immediately.
- **It carries personal accountability.** A certified court interpreter is answerable for accuracy in a way no system can be, and the certification attaches to a named individual.
- **It handles what text doesn't contain** — tone, hesitation, register, the witness who is frightened, the patient who is minimizing symptoms.

==+That's why court interpreting scores 4.3 and document translation 8.8 with identical language skills. The languages are the same. Everything structural about the work is different.==

**The way through isn't to wait for the generalist rung to come back — it isn't coming back.** The entry job most language graduates once started on was general document translation, and that is precisely the 8.8. Entering *beside* it is the move: on the interpreting track rather than the translation one, through court or medical certification, with a domain — law, medicine, engineering — paired to the language, or in an in-house regulated role. That's a set of concrete moves, not a hope — and choosing a program that makes them possible is exactly what the second half of this guide is about.

# How durable is the protection?

| Protection | Strength today | Durability | Where the pressure is coming from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Human trust / accountability** | Strong for interpreting | **Most durable** | Certification attaches to a named person answerable in court. |
| **Regulatory / licensing** | Real and strengthening | **Durable, and expanding** | Language-access law is broadening rather than narrowing. |
| **Physical / embodied** | Real for interpreting | **Durable** | Presence is legally required in many settings. |
| **Judgment under novelty** | Moderate | **Eroding** | Register and nuance resist; standard content does not. |

**The honest read.** ==+Interpreting's protections are genuine and mutually reinforcing — presence, accountability and law. Translation's are almost entirely absent, which is why the score is what it is.==

And one protection here is *growing*: compliance obligations around language access — Title VI requirements, healthcare access rules, court accuracy mandates — are expanding, and each one creates demand for accountable human language work.

# How the role will actually change

| Was the job | Is becoming the job |
|---|---|
| Translating the document | Post-editing machine output — a different and lower-paid job |
| Producing first drafts | Reviewing generated drafts |
| Bulk and repetitive content | Gone, at any professional rate |
| Terminology research | Automated |
| Interpreting in a courtroom | **Unchanged** |
| Being certified and accountable | **Unchanged** |

**One development deserves naming honestly.** ==-Machine translation post-editing has become a substantial share of the translation market, and it pays materially less than translation did.== It is real work and it is a real career for some people. It is also the clearest example in this index of a profession's core activity being converted into a checking function — the thing we describe as a risk elsewhere, already happened here.

# Human strengths that will matter most

1. **Real-time interpreting under pressure** — no draft, no review, immediate consequences. The least automatable thing in this profession.
2. **Certified accountability** — being the named person answerable for accuracy in a legal or clinical setting.
3. **Domain expertise** — law, medicine, engineering. ==+The differentiator is increasingly the subject knowledge rather than the language.==
4. **Cultural and register judgment** — knowing what a phrase means to *this* audience, which is the whole of transcreation and much of literary work.
5. **Reading the person** — the hesitation, the evasion, the distress that the words don't carry.

Notably absent: speed, volume and breadth of general vocabulary. These were the traditional markers of a strong translator and are the fastest-depreciating.

And underneath all five, two traits the score can't grade: ==+curiosity and adaptability.== The languages may be the same across a career, but the tools around them — the CAT platforms, the machine-translation engines, the post-editing workflows — turn over constantly. The linguists who compound are the ones who stay genuinely curious about their subject and re-tool fastest, rather than defending the way of working they trained on. In a field this far through its transition, that flexibility is itself a form of protection — and increasingly it's what the protected end hires *for*: the trajectory toward accountable, domain-deep work, not the raw translation speed that is depreciating fastest.

# Other things to consider about this job

## What's genuinely good about it

**The protected end is genuinely well paid and in demand.**

- BLS projects roughly **6,900 new interpreter and translator openings a year** through 2034
- ==+Live interpreting runs **$80–$250 per hour**; conference interpreting **$600–$1,200 per day**; legal interpreting **$350–$750 per day**==
- Specialist translators (legal, medical, technical) bill **$60–$150 per hour** and have **maintained or increased** their rates while generalists face downward pressure
- Prospects are strongest for Portuguese, Mandarin, Korean, Arabic and Spanish — and ==+"particularly strong for interpreters who combine language fluency with domain expertise in law, medicine or technology"==

**Compliance is a growing tailwind.** Language-access requirements in healthcare, courts, education and government are expanding, and each one mandates accountable human provision.

**And in-house roles offer stability** — government agencies, hospitals and large law firms employ interpreters on salary with benefits and predictable workloads.

**What practitioners report as rewarding:** being the reason two people can understand each other at a moment that matters; genuine intellectual engagement with language and subject matter; and unusual autonomy for those who freelance well.

## What's genuinely hard about it

**The generalist market has collapsed and is not coming back.** ==-Rates at the floor of the market have reached $0.05 per word, and machine translation costs fractions of a cent.== A career built on general translation competence has no economic foundation left.

**Freelancing is the default**, with all that implies: income variability, no benefits, and the business of finding clients being a second job.

**Interpreting is demanding in ways people underestimate** — sustained concentration, emotionally difficult content in medical and legal settings, and no opportunity to correct a mistake.

**And certification is essential but fragmented.** In the US there is no single government certifying body for translators; court and medical interpreting have their own certification regimes, and requirements vary by state.

## Who this work suits — and who it doesn't

Translation and interpreting tend to suit people who:

- **Love languages *and* something else.** ==?The single most important trait now. Language alone is no longer a career; language plus law, medicine or engineering is.==
- **Can hold concentration under pressure** — interpreting is cognitively brutal in bursts
- **Are comfortable with high-stakes accuracy**, where an error has consequences
- **Are self-directed**, because most of this market is freelance
- **Are genuinely interested in people**, not only in text

It's harder for people who:

- Want **salaried stability** — it exists but it is a minority of the market
- Are drawn primarily to **written translation** as a craft. That's the exposed end.
- Need **predictable income** early
- Assume that **fluency is the qualification.** It is the entry ticket.

## What else is moving this market

**Language-access regulation is expanding**, and it is the most reliable source of demand for accountable human language work.

**Domain specialization is the whole game.** The rate data is unambiguous: generalists face downward pressure, specialists in legal, medical and creative fields have held or increased rates.

**And in-house demand is growing in regulated sectors** — healthcare systems, courts and government agencies increasingly employ rather than contract, because accountability is easier to manage.

# The AI-native advantage — what to actually do about it

**The situation:** this profession is further through the transition than any other in this index. The strategic question isn't whether AI will affect it — that's settled — but which version of the work you aim at.

### What an AI-native linguist looks like

**1. Aim at accountable work from the start.** ==+Court, medical, immigration, regulated content. Where an error has consequences, the price differential stops mattering.==

**2. Get certified, specifically.** Court interpreting and medical interpreting certifications are the credential that converts language ability into protected work.

**3. Build genuine domain knowledge.** Legal, medical or technical. This is now the differentiator, and the rate data proves it.

**4. Understand post-editing honestly.** It is a real market and it pays less than translation did. Knowing that going in is better than discovering it.

**5. Learn to direct and verify.** For the content that remains human-supervised, the skill is knowing where machine output fails — false fluency, register errors, terminology that is plausible and wrong.

### Concrete preparation

**Before the degree:** ==+decide whether the goal is interpreting or translation, because they are different careers with a 4.5-point exposure gap.== If it's interpreting, that shapes the whole path.

**During:** pair languages with a substantive second subject rather than treating language as the whole degree. Get interpreting practice specifically — it's a distinct skill from translation and it is trained differently.

**After:** pursue certification early, and target regulated sectors.

# Where language skills can lead later

Interpreting across legal, medical, conference and community settings; specialist and certified translation; localization and transcreation; language-access coordination; international policy, diplomacy and development; language teaching; and any commercial role where the language is paired with genuine domain expertise.

**The pattern in this index holds with unusual force:** ==?the further the work sits from a person in a room and an accountable signature, the higher the exposure.==

# What to look for in a translation or interpreting program

**1. Is there genuine interpreting training?**
Not translation with an interpreting module — actual interpreting practice, which is a distinct skill.

**2. Does it support certification?**
Court and medical interpreting certification is what converts the degree into protected work.

**3. Can students pair the language with a real second subject?**
This is now the differentiator and many language programs still don't facilitate it.

**4. Does the curriculum engage honestly with machine translation and post-editing?**
A program that ignores it is preparing students for a market that no longer exists.

**5. Graduate destinations by type** — interpreting, specialist translation, post-editing, or outside the field entirely.

---

# Questions to ask a program

- How much of the program is interpreting practice rather than translation?
- Does the program prepare students for court or medical interpreting certification?
- Can students combine the language with law, medicine, engineering or another substantive subject?
- How does the curriculum address machine translation and post-editing?
- Where are graduates working at two years — interpreting, specialist translation, post-editing, or elsewhere?

**Red flags:** a curriculum built around general translation practice; no interpreting stream; no certification pathway; no engagement with machine translation; outcomes described as "working with languages."

# The career-hunting checklist

Choosing well is step one. This is what turns a good choice into a good outcome once you're in — worth reading *now*, because the best time to pick a program is knowing exactly what you'll need to do with it. The first list is for the degree and training years; the second is for the job hunt that follows.

## During the degree / training — build toward the protected end

- **Treat interpreting and translation as the fork they are.** The protection lives in the interpreting stream (court 4.3, medical 4.6, conference 5.0), not the translation one that runs up to 8.8. Get real interpreting practice — it is a distinct skill, trained differently, and a translation degree with an interpreting module is not it.
- **Pair the language with a domain while it's still free.** Law, medicine or engineering alongside the language is now the differentiator, and it is far cheaper to build in the degree than to bolt on later. Language alone is no longer a career.
- **Point at the certification, not just the diploma.** Court and medical interpreting certification is the credential that converts fluency into protected work — plan the coursework and the supervised practice around it.
- **Get fluent with machine translation and post-editing as a professional, not a shortcut.** Learn where the output fails — false fluency, register errors, plausible-and-wrong terminology — because verification is the part of the work that survives.
- **Feed curiosity and adaptability on purpose.** The CAT platforms and MT engines will turn over repeatedly; the linguist who re-tools fastest, and stays genuinely curious about the subject, compounds.
- **Keep the frame wide.** Vet the in-house regulated routes — health systems, courts, government — and the domain-paired commercial roles before assuming freelancing is the only shape this career takes. The most crowded lane is general document translation, and it is also the most exposed.

## During the job hunt — aim, don't just apply

- **Target the protected lanes on purpose.** Court and medical interpreting, conference work, certified and regulated translation, a domain-paired in-house role — not "translator, any subject," which points straight at the 8.8.
- **Read the title, not just the offer.** "General translator" and "post-editor" are the automating layer. If you take one, take it as a deliberate stepping-stone with an exit in mind, not as the plan.
- **Don't grab a role just because it's on offer in an exposed lane.** ==-A role being available doesn't make it safe.== An agency still running a large general-translation or post-editing intake may simply be behind the curve — and that's exactly the rung that gets cut or repriced first once they catch up. Availability can be a lagging indicator, not a green light.
- **Prefer accountability, and the in-house room.** A salaried interpreter seat in a hospital, court or agency — where language access is legally mandated — puts you close to protected, stable work sooner than chasing per-word piecework at the market floor.
- **Ask the employer where AI is heading in the role.** Whether they're building the job around accountable, real-time and regulated work, or around cheaper post-editing throughput, tells you if the seat grows or is quietly being hollowed out.
- **Weigh the trajectory over the first rate.** A slightly lower-paid seat that builds certification, domain depth and accountable work beats a higher-volume post-editing contract in a lane being repriced underneath you.

# Bottom line

**Translation contains the most exposed track in this index and a genuinely protected career, and they share a qualification** — which is exactly why the choice in front of you carries so much weight.

The reason is captured in one statistic: ==**73% of enterprises use AI translation internally; only 19% rely on it for external or regulated communication.**== Everything low-stakes has gone, at a cost difference of two to three orders of magnitude. Everything consequential has stayed.

**So the survival condition is not quality — it's consequence.** Human language work persists where being wrong has legal or clinical cost, and nowhere else.

**Which makes the practical advice unusually clear.** ==Aim at interpreting rather than translation.== Get court or medical certification. Pair the language with law, medicine or engineering, because the rate data shows specialists holding rates while generalists lose theirs. And understand that a general translation career has no economic floor left.

And one honest thing worth saying to someone who loves languages. ==+Nothing here says that's the wrong instinct. It says fluency has stopped being the qualification and become the entry ticket — and that being the person in the room, accountable, when two people absolutely must understand each other, is work that is not going anywhere.==

# Questions worth sitting with

*Whether you're the one choosing or a parent thinking it through together, these are the conversations that lead to a better decision. Read "you" as whoever the choice is about.*

## About the work itself

1. **Do you want to translate, or to interpret?** These are different careers with a 4.5-point exposure gap, and most people haven't distinguished them.
2. What made languages appealing — the language itself, or communicating between people?
3. Can you hold concentration under sustained pressure? Interpreting is cognitively brutal.
4. How would you handle emotionally difficult content — a medical consultation, a court testimony?

## Reacting to the findings

5. Document translation scores 8.8 and court interpreting 4.3. Was that surprising?
6. **73% of enterprises use AI translation internally; only 19% for regulated communication.** What does that suggest about where to aim?
7. The guide argues the survival condition is consequence rather than quality. Does that land — and does it apply to other careers you're considering?

## The harder conversation

8. Machine translation costs fractions of a cent per word against $0.08–$0.50 for human work. How do you think about competing with that?
9. Post-editing is a real market that pays less than translation did. Is that acceptable as a fallback?
10. Most of this market is freelance. Have you thought through what that means financially?

## Testing it against reality

11. **The most valuable single action:** observe a court or medical interpreter working, if access can be arranged. It's a completely different job from what most people picture.
12. Find someone working in languages and ask what proportion of their income is translation, interpreting and post-editing.

## Widening the frame

13. The differentiator is now the *domain* rather than the language. What second subject — law, medicine, engineering — would genuinely interest you?
14. Have you considered **in-house roles** in health systems, courts or government? Salaried, stable, and growing with compliance requirements.
15. If languages weren't available as a career, what would you choose? The answer often reveals whether the pull is language or communication.
