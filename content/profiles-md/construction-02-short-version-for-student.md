# So you're thinking about construction management

*Written for you, not about you.*

---

Or maybe you aren't — because almost nobody mentions this one.

We scored twenty-eight careers on how exposed they are to AI. A site superintendent came out at
**3.3 out of 10**.

That's better than **licensed engineering (4.0)**. Better than **architecture (5.0)**. Better
than a business degree (7.6).

==+Median pay is around $106,980, projected growth is 9% — "much faster than average" per the
government's own classification — and there are roughly 46,800 openings a year.==

And your school careers office probably hasn't said a word about it.

---

## The 60-second version

- ==+Site-based construction management scores better than engineering, architecture or business.==
- **No graduate degree. No licence exam in most places.** Progression runs on competence.
- The thing protecting it is unusual: **there are multiple ways in**, and they compete with each other.
- ==-The office roles score 7.4. The site roles score 3.3.== That's a 4.1-point gap on the same project.
- And the AI data centre buildout is driving demand — $100 billion of it in 2026 alone.

---

## Why it's protected

**You have to physically be there.** Walking the site, checking the work, seeing what's actually
happening rather than what's in the report. Construction sites are among the *hardest*
environments for automation — nothing is where the drawing says it is.

**Every site is genuinely new.** Different ground, different team, different problems, arriving
without warning.

**And you're accountable when someone gets hurt.** ==+Safety responsibility attaches to a named
person, and that's not a technical problem to solve.==

---

## The thing that actually makes this career resilient

Here's the argument, and it applies well beyond construction.

Most careers have **one** way in. A law degree. A medical degree. A CS degree into a graduate
scheme. When that route narrows — which is what's happening across this whole index — the whole
profession narrows with it.

**Construction management has at least four.**

- A construction management degree
- ==+Coming up through a trade and moving into supervision — completely normal here, not a
  second-class route==
- Engineering graduates crossing over
- Estimators and office staff moving to site

**And they compete with each other.** No single gatekeeper controls entry.

Plus the entry work itself is physical — an assistant superintendent learns by walking the site
and chasing subcontractors, which can't be automated the way a junior analyst's spreadsheets
can.

==?So when you're weighing any career, ask: how many doors are there? One door is fragile. Four
is resilient even when one of them narrows.==

| Construction project manager | 4.2 |
| Estimator / quantity surveyor | 6.3 |
| Scheduler / planner | 7.0 |
| Preconstruction / document coordination | 7.4 |

==-A 4.1-point gap on the same project.== The person in the mud and the person managing the
document set work on the same building, and one of them is doing work a machine handles well.

**Which matters practically**, because the office jobs are the ones that look like promotions.
Worth knowing that before someone offers you one.

## Where the demand actually is

The industry is genuinely short of people:

- **92% of contractors** report difficulty filling open positions (AGC survey)
- **80%** specifically struggle with salaried roles
- **45%** say shortages are directly causing project delays
- Around **349,000 net new workers** needed in 2026, and 456,000 in 2027

**And the AI buildout is a direct driver.** Reporting suggests tech companies could put
==+upwards of $100 billion into US data centre construction in 2026 alone==, on top of CHIPS
Act megaprojects, healthcare and defence work.

Same pattern as electricians: ==+a career protected *from* AI that's simultaneously being pulled
*by* it.==

---

## One honest thing about that

We'd rather tell you than let the shortage numbers oversell it.

==?The construction hiring rate hit 3.3% in February 2026 — the lowest on record since the
government started measuring it in 2000.==

That sits oddly next to "92% of contractors can't fill positions." Both are true.

What's happening: construction spending growth has slowed, so firms are starting fewer projects
and hiring cautiously — while still being unable to find experienced people for the work they
do have.

**It's a shortage of qualified people in a market that's hiring carefully.** Not the same as a
boom.

The skills shortage is demographic and will outlast the cycle. But if you graduate during a slow
period, it'll feel tighter than the headlines suggest.

---

## What we won't soften

**Construction is cyclical.** It rises and falls with credit conditions and public spending in a
way nursing and teaching don't.

**The hours are long and the weather doesn't care.** A superintendent absorbs pressure from the
client, the designer, the subcontractors, the inspectors and their own firm — often
simultaneously.

**Responsibility arrives early and it's real.** Safety accountability isn't abstract.

**And the median isn't the headline.** ==-Most construction managers aren't on $170,000. The
middle of the market is closer to $105,000== — the average gets pulled up by big metros and
complex infrastructure work.

---

## Where the money actually is

Worth knowing early, because these are choices rather than luck:

- ==+**BIM and platform fluency: +10–15% pay.**== That's the single best skill investment here.
- **Sector matters a lot:** heavy civil $121,060, commercial $120,010, specialty trade $102,140,
  residential $91,150
- **Data centres, healthcare and industrial** carry premiums for complexity
- Certifications (CCM, PMP, OSHA) typically add $5,000–$15,000

---

## Does this actually sound like you?

It suits people who:

- Are **practical and organised** in equal measure — that specific combination is the job
- Are comfortable being **responsible for other people's safety**
- Can **get people who disagree to agree** — most of the job, honestly
- Would rather be **moving than at a desk**
- **Want the thing to exist afterwards**

Harder if you need predictable indoor hours, find conflict draining, want creative freedom
(architecture is closer to that), or need stability through an economic cycle.

---

## What actually matters when picking a course

- ==+**How much of the placement is actually on site**, not in an office?==
- **Named contractor relationships** and real conversion rates
- **BIM taught properly**, as understanding rather than software training
- **What job titles do graduates have at six months** — site or office? That's a 4.1-point
  difference.

---

## Two things worth doing

**Get on a site.** Labouring, work experience, a summer with a contractor. ==+Nothing else builds
the ability to read a site — and that's the thing separating a good superintendent from a
competent administrator.==

**Then talk to both a site superintendent and a preconstruction manager** and ask each what their
day looks like. The contrast between those two answers is basically this whole document.

---

## One last thing

If you like building things and someone has told you the options are "a trade" or "architecture"
— ==+this is the third one.==

It pays more than both at the median. It scores better than both. You don't need a graduate
degree or a licence exam. And you can get there from a trade, from a degree, or sideways from
engineering.

It's the most overlooked good option we found, and the reason is mostly that it doesn't sound
impressive at a dinner party.
