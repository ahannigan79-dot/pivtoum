# So you're thinking about languages

*Written for you, not about you.*

---

We scored twenty-seven careers on how exposed they are to AI.

General document translation came out at **8.8 out of 10** — ==-the highest score of anything we
measured anywhere.==

Court and legal interpreting came out at **4.3**.

**Same languages. Same training. A 4.5-point gap** — the widest split inside any career we
looked at.

So this isn't a question of whether languages are a career. It's a question of *which* one.

---

## The 60-second version

- ==-Document translation is the most exposed work in the entire index.== That's settled.
- ==+Interpreting is genuinely protected — and pays $350–$1,200 a day at the specialist end.==
- **One statistic explains everything:** 73% of companies use AI translation internally, only 19% for regulated work.
- The survival condition isn't quality. It's **consequence**.
- And fluency has stopped being the qualification. It's now the entry ticket.

---

## The statistic that explains the whole thing

Industry research found:

==+**73% of enterprises use AI translation internally. Only 19% rely on it for external or
regulated communication.**==

**That's the map.**

Internal documents, first drafts, understanding a foreign email, bulk content — automated
completely, and it's not coming back.

External communication, regulated content, anything where an error has legal or medical
consequences — still human, and increasingly so.

---

## And here's why the split is so absolute

Look at what the two things cost:

| | Price |
|---|---|
| Machine translation | ==-**$10–$20 per million characters** — fractions of a cent per word== |
| Human translation | $0.08–$0.50 per word |
| Specialist legal/medical translation | $0.15–$0.50 per word |
| Live interpreting | ==+**$80–$250 per hour**== |
| Conference interpreting | ==+**$600–$1,200 per day**== |

==-That's a cost difference of a hundred to a thousand times.== No amount of "but the human is
better" survives that gap for routine content.

**So what's left isn't the work that's done best. It's the work where someone has to be
answerable for the result.**

A mistranslated medication instruction. A contract clause. Court testimony. An immigration
document. ==?In those situations nobody is optimising for translation cost, because the downside
is a misdiagnosis or a wrongful conviction.==

**The transferable idea:** when something becomes nearly free to automate, ask what survives.
What survives is the version where being wrong costs something.

---

## Why interpreting is different from translation

People talk about these together. They're structurally different jobs.

**Translation is text in, text out.** Asynchronous. Reviewable. Correctable. Nobody's in the
room. Every one of those makes it automatable — and those are properties of the *medium*, not
of how hard it is.

**Interpreting is a person, in a room, in real time.**

==+You have to physically be there== — in many legal and medical settings that's actually
required by law.

**There's no draft.** You say the sentence and its consequences start immediately. No review, no
second pass.

==+**You're personally accountable.**== A certified court interpreter is answerable for accuracy
in a way no system can be, and that certification attaches to *you*.

**And you handle what the text doesn't contain** — hesitation, tone, the witness who's
frightened, the patient who's downplaying symptoms.

Which makes translation the most mature example in our whole index — the closest thing we have
to seeing where other professions end up.

---

## What we won't soften

**The generalist translation market has collapsed and it isn't coming back.** Rates at the floor
have hit $0.05 a word. A career built on "I'm good at languages" has no economic foundation
left.

**Post-editing is now a big part of the market** — checking and fixing machine output. It's real
work. ==-It pays materially less than translation did.== Better to know that now than discover
it at 24.

**Most of this market is freelance**, with everything that means: variable income, no benefits,
and finding clients being a second job.

---

## Where the money actually is

The protected end is genuinely well paid:

- **Court interpreting: $350–$750 per day**
- **Conference interpreting: $600–$1,200 per day**
- **Live interpreting: $80–$250 per hour**
- **Specialist translators (legal, medical, technical): $60–$150 per hour**

==+And here's the crucial trend: generalists face falling rates while specialists have held or
increased theirs.==

BLS projects around **6,900 new interpreter and translator openings a year** through 2034, with
the strongest prospects in Portuguese, Mandarin, Korean, Arabic and Spanish — and ==+"particularly
strong for interpreters who combine language fluency with domain expertise in law, medicine or
technology."==

That sentence is basically your career strategy.

---

## So what should you actually do

**1. Aim at interpreting, not translation.** ==+That single choice is worth 4.5 points on our
scale.==

**2. Get certified.** Court interpreting and medical interpreting certifications are what turn
language ability into protected work.

**3. Pair your language with a real subject** — law, medicine, engineering. ==+The differentiator
now is the domain, not the language.== The rate data proves it.

**4. Target regulated sectors.** Healthcare, courts, government, immigration. Compliance rules
around language access are *expanding*, and every one creates demand for an accountable human.

---

## Does this actually sound like you?

The most important trait now: ==?do you love languages **and** something else?== Language alone
has stopped being a career. Language plus law, medicine or engineering is a good one.

It also suits people who:

- Can **hold concentration under pressure** — interpreting is cognitively brutal in bursts
- Are comfortable with **accuracy that matters**, where an error has consequences
- Are **self-directed**, since most of the market is freelance
- Are genuinely interested in **people**, not just in text

Harder if you want salaried stability early, or if you're drawn mainly to written translation as
a craft — that's the exposed end.

---

## What actually matters when picking a course

- ==+**Is there real interpreting training?**== Not translation with an interpreting module —
  actual practice. It's a distinct skill trained differently.
- **Does it support court or medical certification?**
- **Can you pair the language with a substantive second subject?** Many language courses still
  don't make this easy.
- **Does the curriculum deal honestly with machine translation and post-editing?** A course that
  ignores it is preparing you for a market that's gone.
- **Where do graduates work at two years** — interpreting, specialist translation, post-editing,
  or outside languages entirely?

---

## Two things worth doing

**Watch a court or medical interpreter work**, if you can arrange it. It's a completely different
job from what most people picture, and it'll tell you fast whether it appeals.

**Then find someone working in languages** and ask what share of their income comes from
translation, interpreting and post-editing. The proportions will tell you more than any careers
talk.

---

## One last thing

Nothing here says loving languages is a mistake.

It says ==+fluency has stopped being the qualification and become the entry ticket== — and that
being the person in the room, accountable, when two people absolutely have to understand each
other, is work that isn't going anywhere.

Aim there deliberately. It's four and a half points better, and it pays considerably more.
