# So you're thinking about dentistry

*Written for you, not about you.*

---

We scored twenty-eight careers on how exposed they are to AI.

General dentistry: **2.7 out of 10.** Oral surgery: **2.3.**

==+Among the safest work we measured anywhere.== Entry-level software development is 8.1.

So the AI question is settled. **The question worth thinking about is the money**, and it's a
bigger one than most people realize.

---

## The 60-second version

- ==+Dentistry is protected by all four of our factors at once.== That's rare.
- ==-Average dental school debt is $293,900 — and the spread between schools is over $400,000.==
- The income model assumes you'll eventually **own a practice**. Employed dentists earn far less.
- **Dentistry already ran this experiment once**, and the result is the most useful thing in this document.
- And dental hygiene pays a median slightly above a nurse, with a fraction of the debt.

---

## The thing dentistry already proved

This is worth understanding properly, because it applies to every career you might consider.

**Dental lab work was automated more than a decade before anyone was talking about AI.**

Crowns, bridges and dentures used to be made by hand by skilled technicians. Real craft, learned
over years, genuinely artistic. Then CAD/CAM arrived — digital scanning, computer design, milling
machines.

==-Much of that work moved from a bench to a screen and a machine, and far fewer people were
needed.==

**Now look at what happened next:**

**The profession was completely fine.** Dentists still practice. Patients still get crowns.
Demand didn't drop.

**But a whole tier of skilled work contracted permanently**, and the people in it mostly didn't
move up — they moved out.

==?**That's the lesson: automation doesn't usually kill a profession. It removes a *tier* of
it.** And which tier you're in matters more than which profession you pick.==

Two things follow from that, and they're worth carrying everywhere:

**The tier that goes first** is the one with structured inputs and an output a machine can
produce — a crown from a scan, a document from a template, an image from a brief.

**The tier that survives** is the one with the patient, the client, or the accountability
directly in front of it.

And ==+nobody outside dentistry noticed the lab tier disappear.== The same thing is happening
right now inside law, accounting and design, and from the outside it looks identical: the
profession seems healthy, and one layer of it has quietly stopped being a career.

Orthodontics is the fastest-moving track in the profession — aligner design, treatment planning
and remote monitoring are genuinely automatable in a way that a filling isn't.

## Why chairside work is so protected

**You're doing fine motor work inside a wet, confined, moving space belonging to someone who may
be frightened.** That's among the hardest physical problems in medicine, not one of the easier
ones.

**It's comprehensively licensed.** Practice, prescribing and procedures are all legally reserved.

==+**And dentistry runs on trust more than most healthcare**, because a lot of patients are
genuinely afraid.== Trust is what gets people through the door and back again in six months.

---

## Now the money

**The debt is serious:**

- ==-Average dental school debt: **$293,900** (ADA's own survey of dental school seniors)==
- Public schools: **$200,000–$260,000**
- Private: around **$321,000**
- The most expensive programs: **$625,000–$700,000**

**And here's the part that matters most:**

- BLS puts the mean wage for general dentists at **$166,810**
- ==+ADA data puts median net income for practice *owners* closer to **$239,000**==

**That gap is the whole story.** Dentistry's income model assumes you'll eventually own a
practice. If you stay employed, the numbers look considerably worse against that debt.

Worth knowing at seventeen rather than at thirty-two.

**One more statistic that says something.** ADA data indicates around **35% of dental school
costs are paid without loans**, with another 17% covered by family. ==?Dentistry draws
disproportionately from families who can fund it — which means the "average debt" number
understates things for anyone borrowing the whole amount.==

**The practical instruction:** ==+go to the cheapest accredited school you get into. The spread
between programs exceeds $400,000 for exactly the same license.==

## The alternative worth an actual hour

**Dental hygiene**, and the numbers are surprising:

- ==+Median pay **$98,100** — slightly *above* the registered nurse median of $97,550==
- **No night or weekend shifts** (unlike nursing)
- ==+Debt of **$30,000–$80,000** — an order of magnitude below dental school==
- **7% growth**, about 15,300 openings a year
- Scores **3.0** on our index, against 2.7 for dentistry

**Two honest caveats**, because this isn't free money:

==-About **one in three hygienists works part-time** — 28.8% under 32 hours a week, against 53.7%
full-time.== Annual salary figures assume full-time hours a lot of people don't work.

**And benefits follow from that** — health insurance and paid leave are far from universal in
private dental practices, and often depend on full-time status.

**There's also less of a ladder.** Nursing has a longer advancement path; hygiene is fairly flat.

But: **two to three years and $50,000** against **seven-plus years and $293,900**. If what pulls
you is clinical dental work rather than diagnosis, treatment planning and running a business,
that's a comparison worth making properly.

## What we won't soften

**Your body takes a beating.** Dentistry is done in sustained awkward postures, and neck, back,
shoulder and wrist problems are a recognized occupational hazard that ends careers early.

**Patient fear is constant.** A lot of your patients are frightened, and managing that over and
over is more emotionally demanding than people outside the job expect.

**The business is a second job.** ==-Ownership is where the money is, and running a practice
means staff, overheads, insurance negotiation and marketing — none of which dental school
teaches well.==

**And it's repetitive by design.** Same room, same chair, similar procedures. Some people find
that calming. Others find it deadening.

---

## The genuinely good news

**Satisfaction is high**: around **74%** report career satisfaction, and **85% say they'd choose
their specialty again** — at the higher end of healthcare.

**And the hours are better than most of medicine** — around **36 hours a week** on average, with
no routine nights or weekends in general practice.

That combination — protected career, high satisfaction, sane hours, immediate visible results —
is genuinely rare.

---

## Does this actually sound like you?

It suits people who:

- **Genuinely enjoy precise manual work** — this is testable, and it's the core of the job
- Are **comfortable around frightened people**, repeatedly
- **Want to fix something and see it fixed** the same day
- Are **interested in running a business**, or at least willing to be
- Can work in **confined, repetitive physical conditions** for decades

Harder if you want variety of setting, are uncomfortable causing discomfort — which is
unavoidable — or have no interest in the commercial side, since that's where the income sits.

---

## What actually matters when picking a school

- ==+**Total cost. Calculate the debt-to-income ratio yourself.**== The spread exceeds $400,000
  for the same license.
- **Accreditation** — non-negotiable
- ==+**How many procedures do students actually perform** before graduating?== This varies
  enormously and determines how competent you are on day one.
- **Is practice management compulsory?** It's where the income comes from and it's usually
  underweighted.
- **Digital workflow training** — scanning, CAD/CAM, treatment planning

---

## Two things worth doing

**Shadow in two very different practices** — a private owner-operated one and a corporate/DSO
one. The economics and the culture are genuinely different and it'll shape which you aim for.

**Then find a dentist about five years qualified** and ask directly about their loan payments and
whether they'd pick the same school again. Awkward question, valuable answer.

---

## One last thing

If you like the idea of precise hands-on healthcare with sane hours and visible results,
dentistry is a genuinely good answer — and one of the best-protected careers we measured.

Just go in knowing two things: ==+the money assumes you'll eventually own something, and where
you study will affect your finances for twenty years.==

And give dental hygiene a real hour of thought rather than dismissing it. For a lot of people
it's closer to the job they were actually picturing.
