# So you're thinking about physical therapy

*Written for you, not about you.*

---

We scored twenty-eight careers on how exposed they are to AI.

Hands-on physical therapy: **2.5 out of 10.** ==+That's the lowest score of anything we
measured except surgery.==

Entry-level software development: 8.1.

So the AI question here is basically settled. **The question that actually matters is what it
costs to qualify** — and that varies more than you'd believe.

---

## The 60-second version

- ==+Physical therapy is among the most protected work in the entire index.== That part isn't close.
- Occupational therapy 2.6, speech therapy 3.1, respiratory therapy 3.1 — all excellent.
- **The real risk is the debt**, and it depends almost entirely on which school you attend.
- ==-The same degree costs over $300,000 more at some programs than others.==
- And there's an alternative route most people never hear about.

---

## Why it's so protected

**You can't do it through a screen.** Hands on a body. Feeling resistance, guarding,
compensation. Adjusting in real time to how someone actually moves.

We rate this **9.5 out of 10** on physical requirement — the highest rating we give any career
in the whole index.

**It's licensed**, with mandatory supervised clinical hours that can't be automated or offshored.

**It's a relationship over months**, not a transaction. Most of rehab is getting someone to
actually do the work when it hurts.

**And every body responds differently.** The judgment about when to push and when to stop is
made in the moment, with a person in front of you.

---

## One honest caveat

Of our four protections, ==?physical is the one we rank *least* durable over a long horizon —
and this is the career where that matters most.==

You'd practice for about forty years. Licensure and trust move at the speed of law and society;
they'll hold. Physical protection moves at the speed of robotics, which is the one thing
actively aimed at it.

**And there's early evidence worth knowing.** Robotic-assisted rehabilitation is showing
*better* motor outcomes than conventional therapy in some protocols. Not equivalent — better.

Two things keep our score where it is: a licensed clinician still prescribes, supervises and
adapts in every deployment we found, and the research says routine use is still limited.

**The thing to watch** is whether robots move from *assisting* a therapist to *replacing*
therapist hours in structured, protocol-driven rehab. ==?Structured settings are where
automation always shows up first. A stroke patient at home, or an athlete with an unusual
injury, is last.==

## Now the part that actually matters

**The debt.**

- Typical DPT graduate: ==-**$140,000–$170,000** in total student debt==
- Starting salary, outpatient: **$75,000–$95,000**
- So a debt-to-first-year-salary ratio of about **1.7 to 2.0**

For comparison: new physicians are at 0.6–0.9x (way more debt, way more salary). Nurse
practitioners 0.9–1.3x. Pharmacists 1.5–1.8x.

**The APTA — the profession's own body — asked physical therapists for the word they most
associate with student debt.** The most common answers were *"burden," "unfair," "stressful"*
and *"crushing."* Around 28% work a second job on top of their PT role.

---

## The number that should choose your school

Someone analyzed all **206 DPT programs across 151 institutions**.

- Best debt-to-income ratio: **0.82:1**
- Worst: **4.42:1**
- Median: 1.8:1

==-That's a difference of more than $305,000 in total cost. Same degree. Same license. Same
career.==

And it gets worse: federal loans cap out at $20,500 a year, while the average program has a
funding gap of about **$33,647 a year** above that. Over three years that's roughly $95,000 in
**private** loans — which don't qualify for income-driven repayment or Public Service Loan
Forgiveness.

==+**So: go to the cheapest accredited program you get into.** The difference in program quality
is small. The difference in cost lasts decades.==

That's probably the single most valuable sentence in this document.

## The route nobody tells you about

**Physical therapist assistant.**

- **Two-year associate degree** instead of a bachelor's plus a three-year doctorate
- Median pay around **$65,510** versus roughly $80,000–$90,000 for a PT
- Average debt around **$24,000** where it exists at all

Here's the arithmetic: ==+a PTA working during the years you'd spend in DPT school could have
earned close to $290,000 by the time you qualify== — while you'd start with $140,000+ of debt.

**Over a full career the DPT does win.** The income gap compounds and the ceiling is higher.
But it takes many years to close, and how many depends entirely on which school you picked.

**And the AI protection is basically the same** — it's hands-on, physical, licensed work.

If what pulls you is the actual hands-on rehab rather than diagnosis and autonomy, this route
deserves a real look before you commit to the doctorate.

## What we won't soften

==-**Around 70% of physical therapists report moderate to high burnout.**== But look at what
causes it: unrealistic productivity targets, heavy documentation, admin. Not the clinical work.

Which is interesting, because ==+that's exactly the layer AI is removing.== This might be the
career in our whole index where automation most directly fixes the profession's actual
complaint.

**It's also physically demanding over decades** — standing, lifting, transferring people.

**And some patients don't get better.** Sitting with that is harder than most students expect.

---

## Does this actually sound like you?

It suits people who:

- **Want to see progress they can measure** — the most-cited thing people love about it
- Are **comfortable with physical contact** and with bodies generally
- Can **motivate someone who doesn't want to be motivated** — that's most of the job
- Would **rather move than sit**
- Are **patient with slow improvement** — rehab is measured in months

Harder if you need quick resolution, want high early earnings relative to what training costs,
or find it hard when someone doesn't improve.

---

## What actually matters when picking a program

==+**Total cost of attendance. Calculate the debt-to-income ratio yourself.**== Nothing else on
this list comes close.

- **CAPTE accreditation** — non-negotiable
- **How many clinical settings** do you rotate through? Setting shapes the career more than the
  qualification does.
- **Licensure exam first-time pass rate**
- Do they teach **rehab technology and AI documentation tools**?

---

## Two things worth doing

**Shadow a therapist in two completely different settings** — outpatient orthopaedic and
something else, like neuro or paediatrics. They're genuinely different jobs and people rarely
realize that until they're in one.

**Then find someone five years qualified** and ask straight out about their loan payments, and
whether they'd choose the same program again. It's an awkward question and the answer is worth
a lot.

---

## One last thing

Everything above about money is about **which program**, not about **whether**.

The career itself is one of the safest we measured, the demand is demographic and durable, and
==+watching someone walk again, or lift their arm again, or get back to something they thought
they'd lost — very few jobs offer that at all.==

Just don't pay $375,000 for it when someone else is paying $70,000 for exactly the same license.
